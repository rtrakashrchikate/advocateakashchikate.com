<p><strong>In short:</strong> A High Court can quash an FIR or criminal case under Section 528 BNSS (old Section 482 CrPC) to prevent abuse of process. The tests still come from <em>Bhajan Lal</em> (1990) and <em>Neeharika Infrastructure</em> (2021). Judgments in 2025 and 2026 add detail: how Section 528 works after a chargesheet, when a money dispute is only a civil matter, how far courts look into matrimonial allegations, and costs when a settlement ends the case. This page explains what the courts actually said.</p>

<h2>The settled framework</h2>
<p><strong>Bhajan Lal (1990).</strong> The Supreme Court listed examples where a High Court can quash. They include allegations that, taken at face value, disclose no offence; allegations so absurd that no prudent person could accept them; a legal bar to the case; and a case brought with a mala fide motive to take revenge. The Court said the list is illustrative, not exhaustive. The 2026 Supreme Court judgment in <em>G. Saminathan</em> reproduces these categories.</p>
<p><strong>Neeharika Infrastructure (2021).</strong> The Court, in a case from Mumbai, said the power to quash should be used sparingly and "in the rarest of rare cases". The court should not examine whether the allegations are reliable at this stage, and should not stop an investigation of a cognizable offence unless the FIR discloses no offence. A High Court should be cautious in giving interim orders that stop arrest or investigation, and should explain what "no coercive steps" means.</p>
<p><strong>Gian Singh (2012).</strong> Quashing on settlement is different from compounding under the procedure code. The High Court must look at the nature and gravity of the offence. Heinous offences such as murder and rape cannot fittingly be quashed on settlement. Cases with an overwhelmingly civil flavour, such as commercial and family disputes, stand on a different footing.</p>

<h2>Section 528 BNSS and what changed</h2>
<p>Section 528 BNSS saves the High Court's inherent powers "to prevent abuse of the process of any Court or otherwise to secure the ends of justice". It reproduces old Section 482 CrPC, as the Bombay High Court noted in 2026. For FIRs from before 1 July 2024, the old IPC section numbers still appear in the judgments, so they are given here alongside BNS numbers where relevant.</p>
<p>In <em>Pradnya Pranjal Kulkarni</em> (3 September 2025), the Bombay High Court had disposed of a quashing petition as infructuous because a chargesheet was filed. The Supreme Court set that order aside. It said that until a Magistrate takes cognizance, an Article 226 writ can quash an FIR or chargesheet. After cognizance, Article 226 is not available, but Section 528 BNSS can be used to quash the FIR, chargesheet and the cognizance order, if the order is placed on record with proper pleadings and a strong case is made. The petition was sent back to the Bombay High Court to be heard again.</p>

<h2>Civil dispute dressed as a crime</h2>
<p>In <em>G. Saminathan</em> (31 July 2026), the Supreme Court quashed charges of criminal breach of trust (Section 406 IPC, now Section 316 BNS) and cheating (Section 420 IPC, now Section 318(4) BNS) arising from a joint development agreement. It held the complainant was giving a criminal colour to a purely civil dispute. The parties were already in arbitration. The Court applied <em>Delhi Race Club</em> (2024): cheating needs a dishonest intention from the start, and cheating and breach of trust cannot rest on the same allegations.</p>
<p>In <em>Amreeta Rao Singh</em> (15 April 2026), the Bombay High Court quashed Section 406, 409, 420 and 120-B IPC FIRs from a Mumbai police station against one company director. The money was owed by the company, civil suits for recovery had been decreed against the company, and there was no allegation of inducement against her. The Court limited its findings to her case.</p>

<h2>Matrimonial cases (Section 498A IPC, now Section 85 BNS)</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong><em>Mala Awadhbihari Bharadwaj</em> (11 February 2026):</strong> a Pune FIR at Kondhwa police station against a husband and his mother. The Bombay High Court found no dates, times or places of the alleged harassment, no specific role for the mother-in-law, and inconsistencies between the complaint and a later undated complaint. It quashed the FIR, the chargesheet and the Magistrate's cognizance order, calling the cognizance a "rubber stamp" order. This is a single-judge decision on its own facts.</li>
<li><strong><em>Muskan v. Ishaan Khan</em> (6 November 2025):</strong> the Supreme Court set aside a High Court order that had quashed a Section 498A FIR. It held the High Court had in effect conducted a mini trial on credibility, when the allegations of harassment and dowry demand were prima facie made out.</li>
<li><strong><em>Lokesh B.H.</em> (3 August 2026):</strong> the Supreme Court held Section 498A can apply to live-in relationships "in the nature of marriage". It declined to quash, saying the allegations, taken at face value, were a matter for trial.</li>
</ul>
<p>Read together, these show that the outcome turns on what the FIR and chargesheet actually say.</p>

<h2>Consent quashing and costs in the Bombay High Court</h2>
<p>In two September 2026 orders, Justice Milind N. Jadhav allowed settlements to end cases but imposed costs. In <em>Sujit Bhanudas Ghodke</em> (11 September 2026), a cheating and breach of trust FIR was quashed on consent terms, with costs on each applicant paid to the Bar Council of Maharashtra and Goa Advocate Aid Fund. In <em>Niraj Rajaram More</em> (17 September 2026), an FIR from Chikhali police station, Pimpri-Chinchwad, was quashed against one of seven accused. The complainant said he had wrongly named him. The Court held partial quashing is permitted and put the costs on the complainant.</p>
<p>The Court reasoned that criminal law should not be a bargaining chip, and that settlement-based quashing wastes police and court time. It said costs should not be imposed mechanically, and that heinous offences with a wider impact on society are treated differently.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What is the latest judgment on quashing of FIR?</h3>
<p>The most recent covered on this page are <em>G. Saminathan</em> (31 July 2026), <em>Lokesh B.H.</em> (3 August 2026) and the Bombay High Court's <em>Niraj Rajaram More</em> (17 September 2026). New decisions appear often, so check the court websites.</p>

<h3>Which section is used to quash an FIR now, 482 CrPC or 528 BNSS?</h3>
<p>Section 528 BNSS is the new section and replaces old Section 482 CrPC. The tests are the same, and courts still apply <em>Bhajan Lal</em>.</p>

<h3>Can an FIR be quashed after the chargesheet is filed?</h3>
<p>Yes. In <em>Pradnya Pranjal Kulkarni</em> (2025) the Supreme Court held a chargesheet does not make a Section 528 petition infructuous. After cognizance, Section 528 is the route, and the cognizance order should be challenged too.</p>

<h3>Can a High Court quash an FIR if both sides settle?</h3>
<p>It can, in suitable cases, as <em>Gian Singh</em> explains. It will not ordinarily do so for heinous offences. The Bombay High Court has recently imposed costs in consent quashing cases.</p>

<h3>Can a cheating FIR be quashed if the dispute is about money?</h3>
<p>It can be, if the allegations show only a civil dispute. <em>G. Saminathan</em> (2026) quashed charges of cheating and breach of trust on that basis. The court looks at whether dishonest intention existed from the start.</p>

<h3>Will the High Court stop my arrest while it hears a quash petition?</h3>
<p>Not routinely. <em>Neeharika</em> (2021) says interim protection must be given with circumspection and with reasons. A person worried about arrest may need anticipatory bail instead: see <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail</a>.</p>

<h3>Is a 498A FIR against in-laws easily quashed?</h3>
<p>No. It depends on the allegations. The Bombay High Court quashed one Pune FIR for vague allegations (<em>Mala Awadhbihari Bharadwaj</em>), while the Supreme Court in <em>Muskan</em> reversed a quashing order for conducting a mini trial.</p>

<h3>Do I have to go to Mumbai for a quash petition from Pune?</h3>
<p>Yes. The petition is filed in the Bombay High Court at Mumbai, since there is no Pune bench. Consultation can be by phone or video.</p>

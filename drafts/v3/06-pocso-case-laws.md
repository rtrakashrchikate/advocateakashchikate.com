<p><strong>In short:</strong> POCSO judgments deal with how a case is proved, not with whether the offence is serious. The Supreme Court and the Bombay High Court have decided questions on proof of age, the presumption in Sections 29 and 30, bail, quashing, and relationships between adolescents. Each ruling turns on its own facts, and several say so in terms. This page states what each court held and where its reach stops.</p>

<h2>How to read these judgments</h2>
<p>A decision on one set of facts is not a rule for every POCSO case. Some rulings below acquit, some convict, and some refuse relief. None says the offence is trivial. For the sections themselves, see our guide to <a href="/pocso-act-sections-punishment-explained-pune/">POCSO sections and punishments</a>. India's criminal laws changed on 1 July 2024, so some FIRs now cite the BNS and BNSS. Older matters are still tried under the IPC and CrPC. The <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">section table</a> lists the numbers.</p>

<h2>Proof of the child's age</h2>
<p>Age can decide whether the Act applies, and below 16 Section 4(2) carries a 20-year minimum. Section 34(2) lets the Special Court decide age and record reasons. Section 94 of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 sets the order of evidence: the school date-of-birth or matriculation certificate first, then a municipal or panchayat birth certificate. Only if both are missing does a bone-age (ossification) or similar medical test apply.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><em>Jarnail Singh v. State of Haryana</em>, (2013) 7 SCC 263 (1 July 2013, Sathasivam and Khehar JJ.):</strong> The juvenile justice rules strictly apply to a child in conflict with the law. The Court held it just to use the same scheme for a child who is a victim.</li>
<li><strong><em>P. Yuvaprakash v. State</em>, 2023 INSC 676 (18 July 2023, Bhat and Aravind Kumar JJ.):</strong> A school transfer certificate is not a document listed in Section 94(2)(i). The bone test put the girl at 18 to 20, so the prosecution had not proved she was a child, and the conviction was set aside.</li>
<li><strong><em>State of Uttar Pradesh v. Anurudh</em>, 2026 INSC 47 (9 January 2026, Karol and Kotiswar Singh JJ.):</strong> The Court set aside a High Court's direction for medical age tests in every POCSO investigation. Age is a question for trial. A bail court may take a prima facie view from the documents but should not test whether they are correct. The bail already granted was left undisturbed.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limit:</strong> These cases are about how age is proved. They do not decide that a particular child is above or below 18.</p>

<h2>Touch, "sexual intent" and the skin-to-skin ruling</h2>
<p>Section 7 covers touching certain body parts "with sexual intent" and any other act with sexual intent involving physical contact. In January 2021 two orders of the Bombay High Court had required skin-to-skin contact.</p>
<p>In <em>Attorney General for India v. Satish</em> (Criminal Appeal No. 1410 of 2021, 18 November 2021, Lalit, Bhat and Trivedi JJ.), the Supreme Court set those orders aside. Sexual intent is the key ingredient, and the circumstances decide it, such as the relationship, the length and purpose of the contact, and the conduct before and after.</p>
<p>In <em>X v. Rajesh Kumar</em>, 2025 INSC 579 (23 April 2025, Surya Kant and Kotiswar Singh JJ.), a High Court had quashed FIRs against a school teacher on the view that no sexual intent could be inferred. The Supreme Court set that aside, saying the High Court had in effect held a mini trial. It sent the matter to trial and made no finding on guilt.</p>
<p><strong>Limit:</strong> Both go against the accused at the stage where they were made. They show that courts read Section 7 broadly, and that sexual intent is a question of fact.</p>

<h2>The presumption in Sections 29 and 30, and proof at trial</h2>
<p>Section 29 says the Special Court presumes the offence for Sections 3, 5, 7 and 9 unless the contrary is proved. Section 30 presumes a culpable mental state.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><em>Deepak @ JC v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi)</em>, 2026 INSC 1008 (17 September 2026, Mishra and Anjaria JJ.):</strong> The presumptions are not absolute. They operate once the prosecution proves the basic facts, and the burden then shifts to the accused. Judges must still weigh the evidence in the usual way. The Court quoted a Bombay High Court judgment to the same effect (<em>Navin Dhaniram Baraiye v. State of Maharashtra</em>, 2018). On the evidence in that case, it found material contradictions and non-supporting medical evidence, and set the conviction aside.</li>
<li><strong><em>Debraj Dutta v. State of West Bengal</em> (7 April 2026, Sanjay Kumar and K. Vinod Chandran JJ.):</strong> The Court restored an acquittal. The foundational facts were not shown, as the child's account differed between the first telling and the deposition, and the complaint was delayed without explanation.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limit:</strong> The presumption remains in the law. These decisions turn on the evidence in each case, and neither says a child's account is unreliable as a rule.</p>

<h2>Delay in lodging the FIR</h2>
<p>In <em>Debraj Dutta</em>, unexplained delay was one factor among several. In <em>X v. State of Uttar Pradesh</em> (2026), the Court reproduced <em>Bhagwan Singh v. Dilip Kumar</em> (2023), which said delay by itself is not fatal when the complaint explains it, for example by a threat to the child. Delay with a reason is treated differently from delay without one.</p>

<h2>Bail: what courts weigh</h2>
<p>The Act has no special bar on bail. <em>X v. State of Uttar Pradesh</em> (2026 INSC 44, 9 January 2026, Nagarathna and Mahadevan JJ.) shows what a court looks at: the gravity of the allegation and the material collected, and the risk of threats to the child or witnesses, including where they live near each other. A chargesheet does not by itself stop a bail application.</p>
<p>The allegations there were repeated penetrative assault by several persons, with threats and recording. The Court called the plea of consent "wholly untenable" on those facts and cancelled bail. It added that bail is not to be refused mechanically, but must not be granted on irrelevant considerations.</p>
<p>These are the points courts decide on, not grounds that succeed. See <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">bail applications</a>, <a href="/section-439-crpc-section-483-bnss-bail-explained/">Section 483 BNSS</a> and <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail</a>. Under Section 482(4) BNSS, anticipatory bail does not apply where BNS Section 65 or 70(2) is charged.</p>

<h2>Adolescent relationships and consent</h2>
<p>Under the Act, a person below 18 cannot give legal consent. Courts have dealt with relationships between adolescents in different ways.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><em>In Re: Right to Privacy of Adolescents</em>, 2025 INSC 778 (23 May 2025, Oka and Bhuyan JJ.):</strong> A 14-year-old girl had left home and lived with a man, and they had a child. In 2024 the Supreme Court restored a conviction that a High Court had set aside. In 2025 it said that in law it had to impose the minimum sentence. It used Article 142 so that the man did not undergo the sentence, because jail would harm the victim, and said the case was "not going to be a precedent". It ordered the State to support the victim and her child.</li>
<li><strong><em>K. Kirubakaran v. State of Tamil Nadu</em>, 2025 INSC 1272 (28 October 2025, Datta and Masih JJ.):</strong> The accused, convicted under Section 6, married the victim and they had a child. The Court accepted that such a conviction cannot ordinarily be quashed on a compromise. It used Article 142 to quash it on the unique facts, on condition that he maintain his wife and child, and said the order is not a precedent.</li>
<li><strong><em>State of Uttar Pradesh v. Anurudh</em> (9 January 2026):</strong> In a closing note the Court said the law is sometimes used against genuine relationships. It asked the Union Law Secretary to consider a "Romeo-Juliet" clause. This is a request to the government, and the law has not changed.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limit:</strong> The Article 142 orders are exceptional, and the Court said so. In <em>X v. State of Uttar Pradesh</em>, consent was rejected where the allegation was gang assault with threats.</p>

<h2>Quashing and compromise before the Bombay High Court</h2>
<p>The Bombay High Court has decided two such applications in different ways within about six months.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><em>Mirza Aslam Beigh Rashid Beigh v. State of Maharashtra</em>, Criminal Application (APL) 1128 of 2025 (Bombay High Court, 26 September 2025, Joshi-Phalke and Deshpande JJ.):</strong> The accused, then about 27, married a 17-year-old, and they had a child. The Bench declined to quash. It noted that a minor's consent is irrelevant and that the Union's stand before the Supreme Court was that the age of consent should stay at 18. It would not consider such cases until the Central Government clarified its position.</li>
<li><strong>Criminal Application No. 4692 of 2025 (Bombay High Court, 12 March 2026, Chapalgaonkar J.):</strong> The accused was just over 18 and the girl was 16 years 9 months when she left home. She was now an adult, they were married with a six-month-old child, and she and her father gave no objection. The judge quashed the FIR and chargesheet, saying <em>Mirza Aslam Beigh</em> was decided on its peculiar facts, including the age gap.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Limit:</strong> The second order is a single judge's decision on its facts and does not overrule the first. Whether a similar request succeeds depends on the Bench and the facts. See <a href="/latest-judgments-quashing-of-fir-supreme-court-bombay-high-court/">latest judgments on quashing</a> and <a href="/section-482-crpc-section-528-bnss-quashing-fir-bombay-high-court/">Section 528 BNSS</a>.</p>

<h2>Judgments people search as "in favour of the accused"</h2>
<p>Where a conviction was set aside above, the evidence on age or on the basic facts was not proved. Others, like Kirubakaran, rest on facts the Court called unique. None reduces what the Act prohibits, so a family should not rely on a headline.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>What are the main POCSO case laws on age?</h3>
<p>Jarnail Singh (2013) applied the juvenile justice age rules to victims. P. Yuvaprakash (2023) held that the Section 94 order of documents must be followed. State of U.P. v. Anurudh (2026) held that age is for trial, not for a bail court.</p>

<h3>What are the grounds for bail in POCSO cases?</h3>
<p>The Act sets no special grounds. Courts weigh the gravity of the allegation, the material collected, and the risk to the child and witnesses. No ground guarantees bail, and bail granted without weighing these can be cancelled.</p>

<h3>Did the Supreme Court say POCSO presumptions are absolute?</h3>
<p>No. In Deepak @ JC (2026) it said the presumptions in Sections 29 and 30 are rebuttable and apply once the prosecution proves the basic facts. The court still weighs all the evidence.</p>

<h3>Is consent a defence if the girl is below 18?</h3>
<p>The Act treats a person below 18 as a child, and the Bombay High Court has said a minor's consent is irrelevant (Mirza Aslam Beigh, 2025). The Supreme Court has used Article 142 in exceptional cases to relieve a sentence or conviction, and said these orders are not precedents.</p>

<h3>Can a POCSO FIR be quashed if the families settle?</h3>
<p>Settlement does not ordinarily justify quashing for such an offence, as the Supreme Court recorded. The Bombay High Court has refused in one case and allowed it in another on different facts.</p>

<h3>Does a delay in filing the FIR end a POCSO case?</h3>
<p>Not by itself. A delay that the complaint explains, such as by a threat, is not fatal. Unexplained delay can count as one factor with others, as in Debraj Dutta (2026).</p>

<h3>What did the Supreme Court say about "skin to skin" contact?</h3>
<p>In Attorney General for India v. Satish (2021) it held that skin-to-skin contact is not needed. Sexual intent is the key, judged on the circumstances.</p>

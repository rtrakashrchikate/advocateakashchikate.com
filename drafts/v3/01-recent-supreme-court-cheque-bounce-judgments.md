<p><strong>In short:</strong> Between 2025 and 2026 the Supreme Court has decided many Section 138 (cheque bounce) questions. It revised the costs for settling a case, fixed the court where a complaint must be filed, held that the presumption under Section 139 cannot be brushed aside at the start, and held that a demand notice must state the exact cheque amount. It also held that a "security cheque" is not exempt. A few questions are still open. This page explains each ruling in plain words, based on the judgments themselves.</p>

<h2>Settlement and costs (compounding)</h2>
<p>Section 147 of the NI Act allows a Section 138 case to be compounded, meaning settled and closed. In <em>Sanjabij Tari</em> (25 September 2025), the Supreme Court revised the cost scheme from <em>Damodar S. Prabhu</em> (2010). The accused pays the cheque amount, and costs depend on the stage:</p>
<ul>
<li>Before the accused's own evidence is recorded: no costs.</li>
<li>After that, but before the Magistrate's judgment: 5% of the cheque amount, paid to a Legal Services Authority or other authority the court names.</li>
<li>Before a Sessions Court or High Court in revision or appeal: 7.5%.</li>
<li>Before the Supreme Court: 10%.</li>
</ul>
<p>In <em>Gian Chand Garg</em> (11 August 2025), the Court set aside a conviction after the complainant accepted a settlement. It reasoned that the offence is compoundable under Section 147 at any stage.</p>
<p>In <em>Rajeev Khandelwal</em> (4 November 2025), a Bombay High Court order had acquitted the accused after settlement but required costs to be deposited with the State Legal Services Authority. The Supreme Court held that <em>Damodar S. Prabhu</em> is not binding in every case. It set the costs aside because the complainant wanted nothing more and the accused could not pay.</p>
<p><strong>What this means:</strong> an accused who intends to pay should say so early, because costs rise with each stage. A payee who settles should record the settlement in writing and ask the court to close the case.</p>

<h2>Which court can hear the complaint (jurisdiction)</h2>
<p>Section 142(2) of the NI Act decides the court by bank branch. In <em>Jai Balaji Industries</em> (28 November 2025), a bench of Justices Pardiwala and Mahadevan held that for an account payee cheque, the court where the payee's home branch is situated has jurisdiction. A cheque deposited at another branch is treated as delivered to the home branch. The Court called the contrary view in <em>Yogesh Upadhyay</em> per incuriam, meaning wrongly decided without regard to the statute.</p>
<p>On the facts, the evidence stage had already begun before the first court returned the complaint. The Court therefore transferred the case back to that court. So the rule is clear, but cases already at the evidence stage can be treated differently.</p>
<p>In <em>Prakash Chimanlal Sheth</em> (25 July 2025), the Court restored complaints filed where the payee held his account, although the cheques were deposited at a branch elsewhere.</p>
<p><strong>For Pune and Mumbai:</strong> a payee with an account at a Pune branch files before a Judicial Magistrate First Class in Pune. A payee with a Mumbai branch files before a Metropolitan Magistrate in Mumbai (Section 142(1)(c) allows nothing lower than these courts).</p>

<h2>The Section 139 presumption and what rebuts it</h2>
<p>Once the drawer admits the cheque and signature, the court presumes the cheque was for a legally enforceable debt (Sections 118 and 139). The accused must then raise a probable defence. <em>Rangappa v. Sri Mohan</em> (2010, three judges) held that the presumption covers the existence of a legally enforceable debt. <em>Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar</em> (2019, three judges) held that a voluntarily signed blank cheque also attracts the presumption.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sanjabij Tari (2025):</strong> some lower courts were treating Section 138 like a civil recovery suit and asking the complainant to prove the original debt. The Court said this is contrary to the law. It also held that breach of the cash-payment limit in Section 269SS of the Income Tax Act does not make a debt unenforceable. It noted that the accused had not replied to the legal notice.</li>
<li><strong>Ashok Singh (2025):</strong> the complainant need not prove financial capacity at the start. Only if the accused objects must the complainant bring material on it.</li>
<li><strong>Renuka v. State of Maharashtra (2026):</strong> a Mumbai Metropolitan Magistrate had issued process on a Rs. 50 crore cheque. A Sessions Court set aside the process, holding there was no enforceable debt, and the Bombay High Court upheld that. The Supreme Court restored the complaint. At the process stage the court checks the basic ingredients, and the presumption cannot be displaced in a summary way. The accused can rebut it at trial.</li>
<li><strong>Kuntegowda (2026):</strong> a bare denial of any debt does not support the defence. Something probable must be placed on record.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Security cheque: what the Court actually held</h2>
<p>A security cheque is not exempt from Section 138. In <em>Sripati Singh v. State of Jharkhand</em> (28 October 2021), a bench of Justices Bopanna and Shah said a cheque given as security for a loan is not a worthless piece of paper in every circumstance. Once the loan falls due and is unpaid, the cheque matures for presentation. The drawer's defences, such as that the loan was already repaid or the parties agreed to defer payment, are for trial.</p>
<p>The Court also noted that the cheque cannot be presented before the loan or instalment becomes due. In <em>Sanjabij Tari</em>, the questions a trial court may ask the accused list "security cheque only" as one defence to be stated. The Court has not held that such a defence ends the case.</p>

<h2>Legal notice and time limits</h2>
<p>Section 138 requires the cheque to be presented within six months of its date or its validity period, whichever is earlier. The payee must send the demand notice within 30 days of learning of the dishonour. The drawer then has 15 days from receiving the notice to pay. The complaint must be made within one month after those 15 days end (Section 142(1)(b)).</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kaveri Plastics (19 September 2025):</strong> the notice must demand the exact cheque amount. A notice that demanded double the cheque amount was invalid, and a typing error was no defence. Interest and notice charges can be added only after the correct cheque amount is stated.</li>
<li><strong>H.S. Oberoi Buildtech (9 September 2025):</strong> a complaint filed five days late was quashed. The Court held there is no automatic or presumed condonation. The late complaint must come with reasons, and the court must consider them before taking cognizance.</li>
<li><strong>C.C. Alavi Haji (2007, three judges):</strong> a notice sent by registered post to the correct address is presumed served. A drawer who says he got no notice can pay within 15 days of receiving the court summons.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Interim compensation and appeal deposit (Sections 143A and 148)</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Section 143A:</strong> in <em>Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava</em> (15 March 2024), Justices Oka and Bhuyan held that interim compensation, up to 20% of the cheque amount, is discretionary and not mandatory. The court must see a prima facie case, consider the defence, and record brief reasons.</li>
<li><strong>Section 148:</strong> in <em>Jamboo Bhandari</em> (4 September 2023), the Court held that the appeal deposit of at least 20% should normally be ordered, but a court can make an exception for recorded reasons.</li>
<li><strong>Directors:</strong> in <em>Bharat Mittal</em> (18 December 2025), a bench disagreed with two earlier decisions on whether a director can be made to deposit under Section 148 when the company cannot be prosecuted. It referred the question to a larger bench.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Appeal against acquittal: still open</h2>
<p>In <em>Celestium Financial</em> (8 April 2025), Justices Nagarathna and Sharma held that a Section 138 complainant is a "victim" and can appeal an acquittal under the proviso to Section 372 CrPC (Section 413 BNSS) without special leave under Section 378(4) CrPC (Section 419 BNSS). On 12 February 2026, in <em>Everest Automobiles</em>, a bench of Justices Sanjay Kumar and K. Vinod Chandran said it could not agree and referred the issue to a larger bench. No larger bench decision had been located as of 17 November 2026. A complainant considering an appeal should check the current position. For the accused's side, see <a href="/practice-areas/criminal-appeals-suspension-of-sentence/">criminal appeals and suspension of sentence</a>.</p>

<h2>Maharashtra: unlicensed money-lending</h2>
<p>On 29 October 2026 the Supreme Court dealt with a request to clarify its 26 May 2026 judgment in <em>Mansi Finance</em>. The bench noted that, under the Maharashtra Money-Lending (Regulation) Act, 2014, interest-bearing loans against a promissory note by an unlicensed lender are barred and not enforceable. The Court did not decide the point. It left the accused free to argue before the trial court that such a debt is not a legally enforceable debt under Section 138. The earlier 6 April 2026 order it quotes says a private lender should produce a licence at the threshold or show that no interest was charged.</p>

<h2>What a person with a bounced cheque should do</h2>
<ul>
<li>Payee: send a notice with the exact cheque amount within 30 days, wait for the 15 days, and file within one month in the court of your account branch.</li>
<li>Accused: note the date of receipt, reply in writing if you dispute the debt, and appear on summons dates (see <a href="/non-bailable-warrant-cancellation-pune/">non-bailable warrant cancellation</a>).</li>
<li>Both: consider settlement early, since costs rise with each stage.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What is the latest Supreme Court judgment on cheque bounce?</h3>
<p>The most recent ones covered on this page are the 29 October 2026 order in <em>Mansi Finance</em>, <em>Kuntegowda</em> (4 August 2026) and <em>Renuka</em> (7 April 2026). New rulings appear often, so check the Supreme Court website for later ones.</p>

<h3>Is a security cheque safe from a Section 138 case?</h3>
<p>No. In <em>Sripati Singh</em> the Supreme Court held that a security cheque can be presented once the debt is due and unpaid. The drawer can still argue that the debt was repaid or deferred.</p>

<h3>Can a cheque bounce case be settled after conviction?</h3>
<p>Yes. The offence is compoundable under Section 147 at any stage. <em>Gian Chand Garg</em> (2025) set aside a conviction after settlement. Costs may apply, as set out in <em>Sanjabij Tari</em>.</p>

<h3>In which court should the cheque bounce complaint be filed?</h3>
<p>For an account payee cheque, it is the court where the payee's home branch is situated (<em>Jai Balaji Industries</em>, 2025). In Pune this means a Magistrate court in Pune. In Mumbai it is a Metropolitan Magistrate court.</p>

<h3>Does a wrong amount in the legal notice matter?</h3>
<p>Yes. <em>Kaveri Plastics</em> (2025) held the notice must state the exact cheque amount, and a typing error is no defence. The complaint in that case was quashed.</p>

<h3>Can the court order me to pay before the trial ends?</h3>
<p>It can order interim compensation of up to 20% of the cheque amount under Section 143A. The Supreme Court held in <em>Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava</em> that this is discretionary and the court must give reasons.</p>

<h3>Can the complainant appeal if the accused is acquitted?</h3>
<p>The law is unsettled. <em>Celestium</em> (2025) allowed an appeal as a victim without special leave. A 2026 bench doubted it and referred the question to a larger bench.</p>

<h3>Is a cash loan above Rs. 20,000 still a legally enforceable debt?</h3>
<p>The Supreme Court held in <em>Sanjabij Tari</em> that breaching the cash limit in Section 269SS of the Income Tax Act does not by itself make the debt unenforceable under Section 138. The tax law has its own penalty.</p>

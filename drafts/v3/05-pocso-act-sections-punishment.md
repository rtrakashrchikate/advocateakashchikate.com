<p><strong>In short:</strong> A POCSO case is a criminal case under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, where the alleged victim is a child, meaning anyone below 18. Sections 3 to 12 define the offences and set the punishment. Since 16 August 2019 the minimum sentences are higher, and a trial is held before a Special Court. This is a serious matter, and this page explains the law in general terms for families who need to understand it.</p>

<h2>What a POCSO case means</h2>
<p>The Act protects any person below 18 years. Section 2(1)(d) defines "child" that way. It applies whether the child is a girl or a boy.</p>
<p>The Act came into force in 2012. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act, 2019 raised several punishments from 16 August 2019. An offence committed before that date is punished under the older ranges, because Article 20(1) of the Constitution bars a heavier penalty than the law in force on the date of the offence. The Supreme Court applied this in <em>Satauram Mandavi v. State of Chhattisgarh</em> (25 July 2025).</p>
<p>India's criminal code also changed on 1 July 2024. The same act may now be charged under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) as well as POCSO. Our <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">CrPC to BNSS and IPC to BNS section table</a> lists old and new numbers.</p>

<p>Three points are worth noting:</p>
<ul>
<li>The age of the child changes the range. Section 4(1) applies to a child of 16 or 17. Section 4(2) applies below 16.</li>
<li>Before 16 August 2019, Section 4 carried seven years to life and Section 6 carried ten years to life. Section 4(2) did not exist.</li>
<li>Under Sections 4(3) and 6(2), the fine must be just and reasonable and is paid to the victim for medical expenses and rehabilitation.</li>
</ul>
<p>The sections set minimum terms. In <em>In Re: Right to Privacy of Adolescents</em> (23 May 2025), the Supreme Court said that in law it had no option but to impose the statutory minimum. It then used Article 142 of the Constitution on very unusual facts and said the case should not be treated as a precedent. Courts do not ordinarily go below the minimum.</p>

<h2>What makes an offence "aggravated"</h2>
<p>Sections 5 and 9 list the circumstances. Examples include an offender who is a police officer, a public servant, a relative, or someone in a position of trust or authority. Others are gang assault, a child below 12, repeated assault, a child known to be pregnant, and an assault causing grievous hurt or pregnancy.</p>
<p>The 2019 amendment added causing the child's death to Section 5, and added assault during a natural calamity to Sections 5 and 9. It also added Section 9(v), which covers giving a child a drug or hormone to bring early sexual maturity. A charge of aggravated assault raises the minimum sentence, so the FIR and chargesheet should be read for the exact clause.</p>

<h2>Other sections families often ask about</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sections 16 to 18:</strong> Abetment is punished as the offence itself. An attempt can be punished with up to one half of the longest term for that offence.</li>
<li><strong>Section 29 (presumption):</strong> For offences under Sections 3, 5, 7 and 9, the Special Court presumes the person committed the offence "unless the contrary is proved". It does not apply to Section 11.</li>
<li><strong>Section 30 (mental state):</strong> Where an offence needs intention or knowledge, the court presumes it, and the accused may show he had no such mental state. The Act says a fact is proved here only when the court believes it beyond reasonable doubt.</li>
<li><strong>Section 34:</strong> If the accused is a child, the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 applies. If age is disputed, the Special Court decides it and records reasons.</li>
<li><strong>Sections 19 and 21:</strong> Anyone with knowledge of an offence must report it to the police or Special Juvenile Police Unit. Failure to report is punishable with up to six months, or up to one year for the person in charge of an institution.</li>
<li><strong>Section 22:</strong> A complaint made falsely and solely to humiliate, extort, threaten or defame is punishable. The Act exempts a child who gives false information. Whether a complaint is false is for the court to decide on evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Sections 23 and 33(7):</strong> The identity of the child must not be disclosed, including by media. Families should not name the child on social media or in messages.</li>
<li><strong>Section 42:</strong> If an act is also an offence under the IPC (now BNS), the court applies whichever punishment is greater. The Supreme Court explained this in <em>Gyanendra Singh v. State of U.P.</em> (7 March 2025).</li>
</ul>

<h2>How the case moves: FIR, arrest and the Special Court</h2>
<p>Most cases begin with an FIR, often after a report by a parent or the child. The police record the child's statement and arrange a medical examination. Our <a href="/how-to-check-fir-registered-against-you-maharashtra/">guide to checking an FIR in Maharashtra</a> explains how to get a copy.</p>
<p>The police may arrest without a warrant. For Sections 8, 10 and 12, the maximum terms are seven years or less, so Section 35(1)(b) BNSS (old Section 41 CrPC) asks the officer to record reasons for arrest. In some cases a notice under Section 35(3) BNSS is issued instead. See <a href="/practice-areas/police-notice-section-35-bnss/">police notice under Section 35 BNSS</a> and <a href="/rights-when-arrested-india/">your rights when arrested</a>.</p>
<p>Section 28 requires each district to have a Special Court, which is a Court of Session. In Pune, POCSO trials are reported from the Special District and Sessions Court at Shivajinagar. For Mumbai matters, the Special Court for the area applies. A Special Court can take cognizance directly on a police report (Section 33(1)).</p>
<p>The trial is held in camera, meaning in private (Section 37). Questions to the child are put through the judge, and the child is not to be called repeatedly (Section 33). The Act says the child's evidence should be recorded within 30 days of cognizance and the trial finished within a year "as far as possible" (Section 35). Real timelines vary, and I have no verified figure for Pune.</p>

<h2>Bail, anticipatory bail and default bail</h2>
<p>The POCSO Act has no special bar on bail. Section 31 says the bail provisions of the criminal procedure code apply, and treats the Special Court as a Court of Session. Bail is a court's discretion, decided on the facts.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Regular bail:</strong> Section 480 BNSS (old Section 437 CrPC) stops a Magistrate from granting bail where there are reasonable grounds to believe the offence is punishable with death or life. For that reason, bail in Sections 4 and 6 cases is usually heard by the Special Court, then the Bombay High Court under Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439). See <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">bail applications</a> and <a href="/section-439-crpc-section-483-bnss-bail-explained/">Section 483 BNSS explained</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Anticipatory bail:</strong> Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC) allows a request to the Sessions Court or High Court. Section 482(4) excludes arrest under BNS Section 65 (rape of a woman under 16 or 12) and Section 70(2) (gang rape of a woman under 18). Where such a section is added to the FIR, this bar matters. See <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Default bail:</strong> Under Section 187(3) BNSS (old Section 167 CrPC), the police must file the chargesheet within 90 days for offences punishable with death, life or ten years or more, and within 60 days for others. Sections 4 and 6 fall in the first group. Sections 8, 10 and 12 fall in the second. See <a href="/practice-areas/default-bail-undertrial-release/">default bail</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>In 2026 the Supreme Court cancelled bail in a case alleging repeated assault on a child, and held that a bail court should not decide the child's age in a mini-trial. Both are covered in our <a href="/pocso-case-laws-judgments-accused-supreme-court-bombay-high-court/">guide to POCSO case laws</a>.</p>

<h2>Documents a family should collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>The FIR, and later the chargesheet and the statements filed with it.</li>
<li>The remand or arrest papers, and any bail orders.</li>
<li>Records that show the child's age, such as the school certificate and birth certificate. Section 94 of the Juvenile Justice Act ranks these ahead of a bone-age test.</li>
<li>The accused person's own school or birth records, if the accused is young.</li>
<li>Phone, message and call records that relate to the dates in the FIR. Keep them as they are and do not delete anything.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Contacting the child or the child's family to "settle" the matter. This can lead to a bail cancellation or a new charge.</li>
<li>Posting about the case or naming the child anywhere.</li>
<li>Deleting messages or phone data.</li>
<li>Signing statements without legal advice.</li>
<li>Missing the court dates in the Special Court.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>What is the punishment under Section 4 of the POCSO Act?</h3>
<p>Section 4(1) provides imprisonment of not less than ten years, which may extend to life, with fine. If the child is below 16, Section 4(2) applies, with a minimum of 20 years and a maximum of life for the remainder of natural life. For an offence before 16 August 2019, the older range applies.</p>

<h3>What is the punishment under Section 6 of the POCSO Act?</h3>
<p>Section 6 covers aggravated penetrative sexual assault. The punishment is rigorous imprisonment of not less than 20 years, up to life for the remainder of natural life, with fine, or death. Before 16 August 2019 the range was ten years to life.</p>

<h3>What is Section 8 of the POCSO Act, and what is Section 12?</h3>
<p>Section 8 punishes sexual assault under Section 7, with three to five years and fine. Section 12 punishes sexual harassment under Section 11, with up to three years and fine. Section 12 has no minimum term.</p>

<h3>What does "aggravated penetrative sexual assault" mean?</h3>
<p>It means penetrative sexual assault with one of the circumstances listed in Section 5, such as the offender being in a position of trust, a gang, or a child below 12. Section 6 then sets the higher punishment. The exact clause in the FIR matters.</p>

<h3>Is bail possible in a POCSO case?</h3>
<p>The Act does not bar bail. Courts decide it on the facts, including the nature and gravity of the allegation, the material collected, and the risk to the child and witnesses. Applications are usually heard by the Special Court, and then by the Bombay High Court.</p>

<h3>Does the court presume the accused is guilty under Section 29?</h3>
<p>Section 29 says the Special Court presumes the offence unless the contrary is proved. In <em>Deepak @ JC v. State (Govt. of NCT of Delhi)</em> (17 September 2026), the Supreme Court said the presumption is rebuttable and applies only after the prosecution proves the basic facts. The court still weighs all the evidence.</p>

<h3>Which court hears a POCSO case in Pune?</h3>
<p>A Special Court, which is a Court of Session. Reports place Pune's POCSO trials at the District and Sessions Court in Shivajinagar. Confirm the court for your police station from the chargesheet or the court registry.</p>

<h3>Is a consensual relationship between teenagers covered by the Act?</h3>
<p>The Act defines a child as anyone below 18, and the Union government's stand recorded in a 2025 High Court order is that consent of a minor is immaterial. In 2026 the Supreme Court asked the Union government to consider a "Romeo-Juliet" clause. That is a request, and the law has not changed. See our <a href="/pocso-case-laws-judgments-accused-supreme-court-bombay-high-court/">POCSO case-law guide</a>.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> An MPDA order is preventive detention: jail without trial, to stop future acts the authority believes will disturb public order. In Pune, most orders come from the Commissioner of Police, and the detained person must get written grounds within five days. The main court challenge is a habeas corpus writ petition in the Bombay High Court in Mumbai.</p>

<h2>What the MPDA Act is</h2>
<p>The law is Maharashtra Act No. LV of 1981. Many orders still call it the "Maharashtra Prevention of Dangerous Activities of Slumlords, Bootleggers, Drug-offenders, Dangerous Persons, Video Pirates, Sand Smugglers and Persons engaged in Black-marketing of Essential Commodities Act, 1981".</p>
<p>A 2018 amending Act was finally published in the Maharashtra Government Gazette on 9 June 2025 as Maharashtra Act No. XXIX of 2025. It changed the short title to the <strong>Maharashtra Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act, 1981</strong>. It also added people engaged in illegal gambling, illegal lottery and human trafficking.</p>
<p>In Pune, the category used most often is <strong>"dangerous person"</strong> under Section 2(b-1). This means someone who habitually commits offences against the body or property, or offences under Chapter V of the Arms Act. The Act still refers to Chapters XVI and XVII of the old IPC. In recent Pune orders, police also cite Chapters VI and XVII of the BNS for newer offences.</p>
<p>MPDA detention is not a criminal case. There is no trial and no bail application in the usual sense. Release comes when the order is revoked, ends, or is quashed by a court. The ordinary criminal cases against the person continue separately. For those, see <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">bail applications</a>.</p>

<h2>Who can pass the order in Pune</h2>
<p>Under Section 3(1), the State Government can pass a detention order. Under Section 3(2), the State can also empower a District Magistrate or a Commissioner of Police to do so, for a period it specifies.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pune city:</strong> the Commissioner of Police, Pune City.</li>
<li><strong>Pimpri-Chinchwad:</strong> the Commissioner of Police, Pimpri-Chinchwad.</li>
<li><strong>Areas outside the police commissionerates:</strong> generally the District Magistrate.</li>
</ul>
<p>The police prepare a "proposal" that senior officers check. It usually relies on recent FIRs and "in-camera" statements of witnesses too afraid to complain openly. In <em>Rushikesh @ Monya Waghere v. Commissioner of Police, Pimpri-Chinchwad</em> (2025), the Bombay High Court quashed an order because these statements were verified only after the proposal had moved.</p>
<p>The person may be held in a prison far from Pune. In reported Pune cases, detenus were sent to Yerwada, Nagpur and Wardha prisons.</p>

<h2>"Public order" versus "law and order"</h2>
<p>Section 3 allows detention only to stop a person acting against the <strong>maintenance of public order</strong>. Section 2(a) explains this as acts causing harm, danger, alarm or a feeling of insecurity among the general public or a section of it.</p>
<p>Courts keep stressing that a crime against one person is usually a "law and order" problem, not a "public order" problem. In <em>Arjun s/o Ratan Gaikwad v. State of Maharashtra</em> (2024), the Supreme Court quashed an MPDA order against an alleged bootlegger. Relying on <em>Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar</em>, it held that every breach of peace does not lead to public disorder.</p>
<p>In <em>Vidyawant v. State of Maharashtra</em> (May 2026), the Supreme Court quashed another MPDA order. It said a bald, stereotyped statement that activities are "prejudicial to public order" is not enough. There must be real material. In <em>Nilofer Ramjan Shaikh v. Commissioner of Police, Pune City</em> (December 2025), the Bombay High Court found the acts were aimed at individuals and quashed the order.</p>

<h2>What happens after detention: stages and time limits</h2>

<tr><td>Case placed before the Advisory Board</td><td>Section 10</td><td>Within 3 weeks from detention</td></tr>
<tr><td>Advisory Board sends its report</td><td>Section 11</td><td>Within 7 weeks from detention</td></tr>

</table>
<p>The Advisory Board has a Chairman and two members. Each is, or has been, a High Court judge, or is qualified to be one (Section 9). The detenu can ask to be heard in person. However, Section 11(5) says there is no right to appear through a lawyer before the Board.</p>
<p>Article 22(4) of the Constitution bars detention beyond three months without an Advisory Board opinion. In <em>State of Maharashtra v. Balu Waman Patole</em> (2019), the Supreme Court confirmed the 12-month outer limit.</p>
<p>The State can revoke the order at any time (Section 14). After revocation or expiry, a fresh order needs fresh facts that arose later.</p>

<h2>How to challenge an MPDA order</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Representation.</strong> Section 8 and Article 22(5) give the right to make a representation to the State Government. Read the grounds carefully, because they say where to send it. Send it early, in writing, and keep proof of the date.</li>
<li><strong>Advisory Board.</strong> The detenu can put his side in writing and ask for a personal hearing.</li>
<li><strong>Habeas corpus writ petition.</strong> This is filed under Article 226 in the Bombay High Court at Mumbai. There is no Pune bench. A family member can file it; in <em>Nilofer Shaikh</em> it was the detenu's mother. A Division Bench hears these petitions.</li>
<li><strong>Supreme Court.</strong> If the High Court dismisses the petition, an appeal can be taken to the Supreme Court. <em>Arjun</em> and <em>Vidyawant</em> both reached it this way.</li>
</ol>
<p>Magistrate and Sessions courts in Pune do not hear challenges to the MPDA order itself. If you cannot afford a lawyer, see <a href="/legal-aid-india-free-legal-help-maharashtra/">free legal aid in Maharashtra</a>.</p>

<h2>Common grounds on which orders are quashed</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>No real threat to public order.</strong> The acts affect individuals only, as in <em>Arjun</em>, <em>Vidyawant</em> and <em>Nilofer Shaikh</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Ordinary law was enough.</strong> In <em>Arjun</em> and <em>Vidyawant</em>, police had never even arrested the person in the cases relied on. In <em>Aman @ Aman Ibrahim Khan v. Commissioner of Police, Pune</em> (September 2026), a good-behaviour bond under Section 129 BNSS (old Section 110 CrPC) was still running. The Court also rejected the routine claim that he "may get bail" in future. In <em>Banka Sneha Sheela v. State of Telangana</em> (2021), the Supreme Court said a fear of further crimes is a reason to challenge bail, not to detain.</li>
<li><strong>Delay and stale incidents.</strong> In <em>Nilofer Shaikh</em>, 67 unexplained days passed between the last witness statement and the order. In <em>Sushanta Kumar Banik v. State of Tripura</em> (2022), the Supreme Court held that unexplained delay can break the "live and proximate link".</li>
<li><strong>Delay in deciding the representation.</strong> In <em>Sarabjeet Singh Mokha v. District Magistrate, Jabalpur</em> (2021), an unexplained delay of about 60 days was fatal. The State need not wait for the Advisory Board before deciding.</li>
<li><strong>Documents not supplied, or not in his language.</strong> In <em>Dikshant @ Dadu Sapkale v. State of Maharashtra</em> (2025), key papers were given only in English to a Marathi-medium detenu. The Court said failure to give a translation vitiates the order. The Supreme Court held the same in <em>Harikisan v. State of Maharashtra</em> (1962).</li>
<li><strong>Subjective satisfaction vitiated.</strong> Examples include contradictory statements in the grounds (<em>Aman Khan</em>), reliance on a crime the person was not accused in (<em>Sapkale</em>), or relevant bail orders kept from the authority (<em>Sushanta Kumar Banik</em>).</li>
</ul>
<p>One caution: Section 5A says an order resting on several grounds survives even if one ground is bad. A challenge needs a defect that goes to the root of the order.</p>

<h2>Documents to collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>The detention order, the committal order, and the grounds of detention, in English and Marathi.</li>
<li>The full set of documents supplied with the grounds, and the date each was received.</li>
<li>Copies of every FIR mentioned, with arrest and bail orders.</li>
<li>Any chapter case bond or externment order mentioned in the grounds.</li>
<li>A copy of the representation, proof of when it was handed over, and any reply.</li>
<li>Approval, Advisory Board and confirmation papers, once received.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Realistic time</h2>
<p>The Act's own deadlines are in the table above. Court time varies. In the three Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad judgments cited here, the High Court decided seven to nine months after the order was passed. Since the maximum is 12 months, act early.</p>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Filing a bail application in the Sessions Court against the MPDA order. That is not the remedy.</li>
<li>Waiting for the Advisory Board before sending a representation.</li>
<li>Sending a vague representation, or keeping no proof of when it was sent.</li>
<li>Not noting the exact date the grounds and documents were served.</li>
<li>Signing papers in a language the detenu does not understand without asking for a translation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>MPDA kya hai? एमपीडीए कारवाई म्हणजे काय?</h3>
<p>It is preventive detention (स्थानबद्धता) under the Maharashtra Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act, 1981. Police use it against people they label as dangerous persons, bootleggers or drug-offenders. It is not a sentence. It is meant to stop future acts against public order.</p>
<h3>Can someone get bail in an MPDA case?</h3>
<p>No regular bail application lies against an MPDA detention order. The remedies are a representation, the Advisory Board and a habeas corpus petition in the High Court. The State can also release a detenu temporarily under Section 15.</p>
<h3>How long can MPDA detention last?</h3>
<p>The maximum is 12 months from the date of detention, under Section 13. The State can revoke it earlier. The Advisory Board must report within seven weeks of detention.</p>
<h3>Who can file a habeas corpus petition for an MPDA detenu?</h3>
<p>The detenu or a close family member can file it. In one Pune case, the detenu's mother filed it. It is filed in the Bombay High Court at Mumbai, as there is no Pune bench.</p>
<h3>Can police pass a second MPDA order after release?</h3>
<p>Section 14(2) allows a fresh order after revocation or expiry only where fresh facts have arisen. A copy of the old order on the same facts is open to challenge.</p>
<h3>Can a lawyer argue before the MPDA Advisory Board?</h3>
<p>Section 11(5) says the detenu has no right to appear through a lawyer before the Board. He can ask to be heard in person. A lawyer can help prepare the written representation.</p>

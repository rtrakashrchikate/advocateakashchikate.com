<p><strong>In short:</strong> Abetment of suicide (Section 108 BNS, old Section 306 IPC) and dowry death (Section 80 BNS, old Section 304B IPC) are non-bailable offences tried by the Sessions Court. A death, however tragic, does not by itself make anyone guilty. The prosecution must prove a specific act of instigation or help, done with intent, or, for dowry death, dowry-linked cruelty "soon before" the death. A family member named in such an FIR in Pune can apply for anticipatory bail and, where the FIR does not show these ingredients, can ask the Bombay High Court to quash it.</p>

<p>This page is for families who are grieving a death and also facing a police case. It explains the law calmly, so you can make careful decisions.</p>

<h2>Which sections are usually applied</h2>
<p>India's criminal laws changed on 1 July 2024. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced the IPC, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) replaced the Evidence Act. Older FIRs still use the old numbers. You can compare them on our <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">IPC to BNS section table</a>.</p>

<p>All three are cognizable, so police can register an FIR and investigate. Section 85 is cognizable when the woman, or a relative by blood, marriage or adoption, gives the information. When Section 85 is combined with Section 108 or Section 80, the whole case is usually tried by the Sessions Court. Charges under the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 are also sometimes added.</p>

<h2>What the prosecution has to prove</h2>
<h3>Abetment of suicide (Section 108 BNS)</h3>
<p>"Abetment" means instigating, conspiring, or intentionally aiding. The Supreme Court has repeatedly said three things matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A positive act of instigation or help.</strong> In <em>M. Arjunan v. State</em> (decided December 2018, reported in (2019) 3 SCC 315), the Court said the ingredients are the abetment itself and the accused's intention to aid or instigate the suicide. Abusive words alone were not enough.</li>
<li><strong>A guilty mind (mens rea).</strong> In <em>Gurcharan Singh v. State of Punjab</em> (2020), the Court held that intention to abet cannot be assumed. It must be clearly visible from the evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Closeness in time and cause.</strong> In <em>Nipun Aneja v. State of Uttar Pradesh</em> (2024), the Court quashed a case against company officers. It held that the offence requires direct and alarming encouragement or incitement, and that ordinary workplace pressure is different from abetment.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Dowry death (Section 80 BNS)</h3>
<p>Dowry death applies when a woman dies by burns, bodily injury, or otherwise than under normal circumstances, within seven years of marriage. It must also be shown that soon before her death, her husband or his relative subjected her to cruelty or harassment linked to a dowry demand. A suicide can fall within this definition.</p>
<p>In <em>Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana</em> (2021), the Supreme Court said "soon before" does not mean "immediately before". However, there must be a proximate and live link between the dowry-related cruelty and the death.</p>

<h2>The legal presumptions under the BSA</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Section 117 BSA (old Section 113A Evidence Act):</strong> If a married woman dies by suicide within seven years of marriage, and her husband or his relative subjected her to cruelty, the court <em>may</em> presume abetment, having regard to all the circumstances. This is discretionary. In <em>Naresh Kumar v. State of Haryana</em> (2024), the Supreme Court acquitted a husband. It held that the presumption cannot replace proof of cruelty or harassment.</li>
<li><strong>Section 118 BSA (old Section 113B Evidence Act):</strong> If it is shown that, soon before her death, the woman was subjected by a person to cruelty or harassment for dowry, the court <em>shall</em> presume that person caused the dowry death. This is stronger, but it can be rebutted. It also only arises after the prosecution first proves the basic facts.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both presumptions apply only to a married woman's death. They do not apply to a workplace or business suicide.</p>

<h2>Cases with a suicide note</h2>
<p>A suicide note naming someone is serious evidence, but it is not automatic proof. Courts read the note with the rest of the evidence. They ask whether it shows instigation with intent, or only distress, anger or financial pressure.</p>
<p>In <em>M. Arjunan</em>, the Supreme Court found that the note showed financial difficulty but did not show instigation. In <em>Mahendra Awase v. State of Madhya Pradesh</em> (2025), a note named a person who had been pressing for repayment of a loan. The Court discharged him, holding that harassment over repayment did not, on those facts, amount to abetment. This is relevant where an employer, business partner or lender is accused.</p>

<h2>What happens after the FIR</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Inquest and post-mortem.</strong> Police hold an inquest under Section 194 BNSS (old Section 174 CrPC). If a woman dies by suicide within seven years of marriage, the law requires the body to be sent for post-mortem examination.</li>
<li><strong>Statements and FIR.</strong> The deceased's family usually gives statements. The FIR may name the husband, parents-in-law, siblings, or others.</li>
<li><strong>Arrest risk.</strong> In <em>Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar</em> (2014), the Supreme Court restricted automatic arrests in 498A cases and offences punishable up to seven years. That protection does not extend to Section 108 or Section 80, which carry higher punishment.</li>
<li><strong>Chargesheet and committal.</strong> After investigation, the chargesheet goes to the Magistrate. Because these offences are sessions-triable, the case is then committed to the Sessions Court for trial. See <a href="/practice-areas/sessions-trial-defence/">sessions trial defence</a>.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Your options, and which court</h2>
<h3>Anticipatory bail</h3>
<p>Anticipatory bail is under Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC). It can be filed in the Pune District and Sessions Court at Shivajinagar or in the Bombay High Court at Mumbai. There is no Pune bench of the High Court.</p>
<p>For relatives who lived in a different house or city, courts look closely at whether the FIR gives any specific act by that person. In <em>Kahkashan Kausar v. State of Bihar</em> (2022), the Supreme Court quashed an FIR against in-laws because the allegations were general and omnibus. More on the process: <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail in Pune</a>.</p>

<h3>Regular bail after arrest</h3>
<p>After arrest, bail is sought under Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC) in the Sessions Court and, if refused, in the High Court.</p>

<h3>Default bail</h3>
<p>Under Section 187(3) BNSS (old Section 167(2) CrPC), an accused in custody is entitled to bail if the chargesheet is not filed in time. The limit is 90 days for offences punishable with death, life, or not less than ten years, and 60 days for others. Dowry death falls in the 90-day category. For an offence that only "may extend to" ten years, the Supreme Court in <em>Rajeev Chaudhary v. State (NCT of Delhi)</em> (2001) applied the 60-day period. The period depends on every section in your FIR. See <a href="/practice-areas/default-bail-undertrial-release/">default bail</a>.</p>

<h3>FIR quashing and discharge</h3>
<p>If the FIR, read as it stands, does not show instigation or intent, a petition under Section 528 BNSS (old Section 482 CrPC) can be filed in the Bombay High Court. Read more on <a href="/fir-quashing-bombay-high-court-pune/">FIR quashing in the Bombay High Court</a>. After the chargesheet, a <a href="/practice-areas/discharge-application/">discharge application</a> can be made before charges are framed, as happened in <em>Mahendra Awase</em>.</p>

<h2>Documents to collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>Copy of the FIR, and later the inquest panchnama, post-mortem report and chargesheet.</li>
<li>Proof of the date of marriage (marriage certificate, invitation card, photographs).</li>
<li>Proof of separate residence for relatives: rent agreement, electricity bills, ID address, or employment records in another city.</li>
<li>Messages, call records and emails with the deceased, kept unaltered on the original phone.</li>
<li>Bank records of any money given or received, including loan agreements and repayment records in creditor cases.</li>
<li>In workplace cases: HR records, emails and company policies.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Realistic time</h2>
<p>There is no fixed timeline. Anticipatory bail hearings depend on the court's listing and whether police are asked to file a reply. A Sessions trial depends on the number of witnesses, forensic reports and adjournments. Your advocate can give an estimate once the papers are seen. Fees also depend on the court and stage; see <a href="/criminal-lawyer-fees-pune/">criminal lawyer fees in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Do not contact or pressure the deceased's family or witnesses.</strong> This can lead to a new case or the cancellation of bail.</li>
<li><strong>Do not delete chats or reset phones.</strong> The same data may help your defence.</li>
<li><strong>Do not post about the death or the case on social media.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Do not assume that living separately protects you.</strong> Seek legal advice early, even if your name appears only in general terms.</li>
<li><strong>Do not ignore police notices or court dates.</strong> Respond through your advocate.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Is Section 108 BNS bailable?</h3>
<p>No. Section 108 BNS (old Section 306 IPC) is cognizable and non-bailable, and is tried by the Sessions Court. Bail is not a matter of right, but courts can grant bail or anticipatory bail on the facts.</p>

<h3>Is Section 80 BNS bailable? 304B me bail milti hai kya?</h3>
<p>Section 80 BNS (old Section 304B IPC) is non-bailable and sessions-triable. The minimum punishment is seven years. Bail is still possible, but courts examine the evidence of dowry-related cruelty soon before death very carefully.</p>

<h3>306 IPC case me anticipatory bail kaise milti hai?</h3>
<p>An application under Section 482 BNSS (old 438 CrPC) is filed in the Sessions Court or the Bombay High Court. The court looks at the specific role alleged, any suicide note, the closeness in time, and the risk of tampering with evidence.</p>

<h3>My name is in the suicide note. Will I be convicted?</h3>
<p>Not automatically. The Supreme Court has held that a note must be read with the other evidence. It must show instigation with intent, not just the deceased's distress or a dispute with the person named.</p>

<h3>Can parents-in-law or a sister-in-law living in another city be charged?</h3>
<p>They can be named, but courts look for specific allegations against each person. Where allegations are general, courts have quashed proceedings against relatives, as in <em>Kahkashan Kausar</em>.</p>

<h3>Can an employer or a lender be charged with abetment of suicide?</h3>
<p>Yes, Section 108 BNS applies to anyone, not only family. But ordinary workplace pressure or a demand to repay money is not abetment by itself, as the Supreme Court held in <em>Nipun Aneja</em> and <em>Mahendra Awase</em>.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> Bail in an NDPS case depends mainly on the quantity of drug the police say they seized. For small and intermediate quantity, the ordinary bail rules apply. For commercial quantity, Section 37 of the NDPS Act allows bail only if the court finds reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty and is not likely to offend while on bail. Defects in the search, long custody and the 180-day default bail rule are the main routes to bail in serious cases.</p>

<h2>Why quantity decides almost everything</h2>
<p>The NDPS Act sorts every seizure into three bands. The Central Government fixes the figures for each drug in a notification (S.O. 1055(E) dated 19 October 2001, as amended).</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Small quantity:</strong> less than the "small" figure. Punishment for possession can go up to one year of rigorous imprisonment, or a fine up to ₹10,000, or both.</li>
<li><strong>Intermediate quantity:</strong> more than small but less than commercial. Punishment can go up to ten years and a fine up to ₹1 lakh.</li>
<li><strong>Commercial quantity:</strong> more than the "commercial" figure. Punishment is a minimum of ten years and up to twenty years, plus a fine.</li>
</ul>
<p>Here are the official figures for common drugs:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Drug</th><th>Small quantity (less than)</th><th>Commercial quantity (more than)</th></tr>
<tr><td>Ganja</td><td>1,000 grams (1 kg)</td><td>20 kg</td></tr>
<tr><td>Charas / hashish</td><td>100 grams</td><td>1 kg</td></tr>
<tr><td>Heroin (smack, "brown sugar")</td><td>5 grams</td><td>250 grams</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cocaine</td><td>2 grams</td><td>100 grams</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mephedrone (MD, "meow meow")</td><td>2 grams</td><td>50 grams</td></tr>
<tr><td>MDMA (ecstasy)</td><td>0.5 gram</td><td>10 grams</td></tr>
<tr><td>Amphetamine</td><td>2 grams</td><td>50 grams</td></tr>

<p>Two points matter. First, the Supreme Court held in <em>Hira Singh v. Union of India</em> (2020) that the whole weight of a mixture counts, not only the pure drug content. The Court agreed in 2025 to hear a challenge to that view. Second, MD limits are low: just over 50 grams is commercial quantity.</p>
<p>In February 2024, Pune City Police Crime Branch reported large mephedrone seizures from a factory in Kurkumbh MIDC and godowns in Vishrantwadi. For penalties, see <a href="/drug-offences-ndps-act-pune-penalties/">drug offences and penalties under the NDPS Act in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Section 37: the twin conditions for commercial quantity</h2>
<p>Section 37 applies to commercial quantity cases. It also applies to offences under Sections 19, 24 and 27A (which cover cultivators embezzling opium, illegal external dealings, and financing or harbouring offenders). In these cases, bail can be granted only if:</p>
<ol>
<li>the Public Prosecutor has been given a chance to oppose the bail application; and</li>
<li>if the Prosecutor opposes it, the court is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is <strong>not guilty</strong> and is <strong>not likely to commit any offence</strong> while on bail.</li>
</ol>
<p>In <em>Union of India v. Md. Nawaz Khan</em> (2021), the Supreme Court cancelled a bail order. The High Court had granted bail mainly because no drugs were found on the accused personally. The Supreme Court said the court must apply the Section 37 conditions to the whole record.</p>
<p>In <em>Mohd. Muslim @ Hussain v. State (NCT of Delhi)</em> (2023), the Supreme Court said the court's satisfaction is only <em>prima facie</em>, based on a broad reading of the material. It is not a mini-trial. The Court also held that undue delay in trial is a ground for bail that Section 37 does not block. The accused there had been in custody for over seven years.</p>

<h2>Search and seizure rules: Sections 41, 42, 43, 50 and 52A</h2>
<p>The Act gives the police wide powers but sets strict procedures. Lapses can be raised at bail and at trial.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Section 41:</strong> search warrants by a Magistrate, or authorisation by an empowered gazetted officer.</li>
<li><strong>Section 42:</strong> search of a building, vehicle or enclosed place on information. The officer must write down the information. A copy must go to his immediate superior within 72 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Section 43:</strong> seizure and arrest in a public place without a warrant. A "public place" includes public transport, hotels and shops.</li>
<li><strong>Section 50:</strong> before a <em>personal</em> search, the person must be told of the right to be searched before a gazetted officer or a Magistrate. A five-judge bench in <em>Vijaysinh Chandubha Jadeja v. State of Gujarat</em> (2010) held this duty is mandatory. In <em>Ranjan Kumar Chadha v. State of Himachal Pradesh</em> (2023), the Court held Section 50 covers a search of the body, not a bag the person carries.</li>
<li><strong>Section 52A:</strong> seized drugs must be listed in an inventory, and samples drawn and certified before a Magistrate. In <em>Union of India v. Mohanlal</em> (2016), the Supreme Court stressed that sampling must be done in the Magistrate's presence. Detailed rules now exist in the NDPS (Seizure, Storage, Sampling and Disposal) Rules, 2022.</li>
</ul>
<p>Be realistic about these grounds. In <em>Narcotics Control Bureau v. Kashif</em> (2024), the Supreme Court held that a delay or lapse in Section 52A compliance is not, by itself, a ground for bail. Such defects are usually argued together with other weaknesses in the case.</p>

<h2>Section 67 statements after Tofan Singh</h2>
<p>NDPS officers often record a statement under Section 67. In <em>Tofan Singh v. State of Tamil Nadu</em> (2020), the Supreme Court held that such a statement cannot be used as a confession at trial. NDPS officers with Section 53 powers count as "police officers". So confessions made to them are barred, as with any police confession (Section 25 of the Evidence Act, now Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam). This matters when the only link to the accused is his own statement, or a co-accused's.</p>

<h2>Default bail: the 180-day rule under Section 36A(4)</h2>
<p>Normally, the police must file a chargesheet within 60 or 90 days under Section 187 BNSS (old Section 167 CrPC). If they fail, the accused can claim default bail.</p>
<p>For commercial quantity cases and offences under Sections 19, 24 and 27A, Section 36A(4) of the NDPS Act raises this limit to <strong>180 days</strong>. The Special Court can extend it up to <strong>one year</strong>. But only on a report of the <em>Public Prosecutor</em> showing the progress of the investigation and specific reasons for keeping the accused in custody. A request from the police alone is not enough.</p>
<p>This right comes from the time limit, not from the merits. The application must be filed before the chargesheet comes in. See <a href="/practice-areas/default-bail-undertrial-release/">default bail and undertrial release</a>.</p>

<h2>Which court in Pune?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Magistrate court:</strong> under Section 36A, offences punishable up to three years (most small quantity and consumption cases) can be tried by a Magistrate. Bail is sought there under Section 480 BNSS (old Section 437 CrPC).</li>
<li><strong>Special NDPS Court:</strong> offences punishable with more than three years (intermediate and commercial quantity) are triable only by a Special Court. In Pune, this is a Special Judge at the Pune District and Sessions Court, Shivajinagar. Remand and bail in these cases come before that court.</li>
<li><strong>Bombay High Court, Mumbai:</strong> if the Special Court refuses bail, a bail application lies to the High Court under Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC). There is no Pune bench of the High Court. See <a href="/bail-rejected-sessions-court-pune-what-next/">bail rejected in Pune: what next</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before arrest, anticipatory bail may be sought under Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC). See <a href="/how-to-get-anticipatory-bail-pune/">how anticipatory bail works in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Consumption cases (Section 27)</h2>
<p>Using drugs is a separate, lighter offence under Section 27. For cocaine, morphine, heroin and certain other drugs, the punishment is up to one year, or a fine up to ₹20,000, or both. For other drugs, including ganja, it is up to six months, or a fine up to ₹10,000, or both.</p>
<p>Under Section 64A, an addict charged with consumption or a small quantity offence who volunteers for de-addiction at a government or recognised centre is protected from prosecution. The protection can be withdrawn if treatment is not completed.</p>

<h2>Documents to collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>FIR, arrest memo and remand orders (see <a href="/police-kothadi-nyayalayin-kothadi-farak/">police vs judicial custody</a>).</li>
<li>Seizure panchnama and any Section 50 notice.</li>
<li>Sampling record and the FSL report, once given.</li>
<li>Exact dates: arrest, first remand and chargesheet. These decide default bail.</li>
<li>Proof of address, job, family and health, for bail conditions.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Realistic time</h2>
<p>There is no fixed time. Small quantity bail can be quick. Commercial quantity bail often waits for the chargesheet, the FSL report or a High Court hearing.</p>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Signing a statement or panchnama without reading it.</li>
<li>Assuming the 90-day rule applies. In commercial quantity cases it is 180 days.</li>
<li>Missing the default bail date. The right is lost once the chargesheet is filed.</li>
<li>Relying on one procedural defect alone. Courts look at the whole record.</li>
<li>Ignoring the weight in the panchnama. A few grams of MD can change the band.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>NDPS case me bail kaise milti hai?</h3>
<p>It depends on quantity. Small and intermediate quantity cases follow normal bail rules. Commercial quantity needs Section 37's twin conditions, or default bail after 180 days.</p>

<h3>Is a small quantity NDPS case bailable?</h3>
<p>Small quantity possession carries up to one year, so it is generally treated as bailable. Every NDPS offence is still cognizable, so police can arrest without a warrant.</p>

<h3>Can I get bail in a ganja case in Pune?</h3>
<p>Below 1 kg, bail is usually not difficult. Between 1 kg and 20 kg, the court applies ordinary bail principles, though the punishment can reach ten years. Above 20 kg, Section 37 applies.</p>

<h3>MD drugs case me bail milegi kya (Pune)?</h3>
<p>MD has a commercial limit of only 50 grams. More than that brings in Section 37. Bail then usually turns on search defects, the FSL report, the evidence against that person, long custody or default bail.</p>

<h3>How many days to file a chargesheet in an NDPS case?</h3>
<p>For commercial quantity and Sections 19, 24 and 27A, it is 180 days. On the Public Prosecutor's report, the Special Court can extend this up to one year. Other NDPS cases follow the normal 60 or 90 days under Section 187 BNSS.</p>

<h3>Can bail be granted in a commercial quantity case after long custody?</h3>
<p>Yes, in suitable cases. In <em>Mohd. Muslim</em> (2023), the Supreme Court granted bail after over seven years in custody. It held that undue delay in trial is a ground Section 37 does not block.</p>

<h3>What if police did not follow Section 50?</h3>
<p>If drugs were found in a personal search without the person being told of this right, the recovery can be challenged. Section 50 does not cover a bag search. Such defects are decided finally at trial.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> When a road accident causes a death in Pune, police usually register the case under Section 106(1) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), which replaced Section 304A IPC. It carries up to five years and fine, and the BNSS First Schedule lists it as bailable. The stricter hit-and-run clause, Section 106(2), has never been brought into force. Cases become far more serious only when police allege culpable homicide under Section 105 BNS (old Section 304 IPC), for example in some drunk-driving cases.</p>

<h2>Which sections apply in a fatal or injury accident case</h2>
<p>Since 1 July 2024, the BNS has replaced the IPC and the BNSS has replaced the CrPC. The accident sections you are likely to see in an FIR are these:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Offence</th><th>New section (BNS)</th><th>Old section (IPC)</th><th>Maximum punishment</th><th>Bailable?</th></tr>
<tr><td>Rash driving on a public road</td><td>281</td><td>279</td><td>6 months, or fine up to Rs 1,000, or both</td><td>Bailable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Endangering life or safety of others</td><td>125</td><td>336</td><td>3 months, or fine up to Rs 2,500, or both</td><td>Bailable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Same act causing hurt</td><td>125(a)</td><td>337</td><td>6 months, or fine up to Rs 5,000, or both</td><td>Bailable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Same act causing grievous hurt</td><td>125(b)</td><td>338</td><td>3 years, or fine up to Rs 10,000, or both</td><td>Bailable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Causing death by negligence</td><td>106(1)</td><td>304A</td><td>5 years and fine (old law: 2 years)</td><td>Bailable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Culpable homicide not amounting to murder</td><td>105</td><td>304</td><td>Up to life imprisonment, or 5 to 10 years, and fine</td><td>Non-bailable</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Bailability comes from the First Schedule of the BNSS, not from the BNS itself. All the offences above are cognizable, which means police can register an FIR and arrest without a warrant. For a full conversion list, see our <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">IPC to BNS and CrPC to BNSS section table</a>.</p>

<h2>Section 106(2) BNS: the hit-and-run clause is not in force</h2>
<p>Section 106(2) says that a driver who causes death by rash and negligent driving and escapes without reporting to the police or a Magistrate soon after can get up to ten years and fine. The First Schedule lists it as non-bailable.</p>
<p>However, this clause has never come into force. The Central Government's notification of 23 February 2024 brought the BNS into force from 1 July 2024 "except" Section 106(2). In January 2024, after a nationwide truckers' strike, the Home Ministry said the clause would be invoked only after consulting the All India Motor Transport Congress.</p>
<p>Courts have confirmed this position. In December 2024, the Gauhati High Court directed Assam Police not to register cases under Section 106(2) because it was not in force. In July 2026, the Delhi High Court dismissed a PIL seeking its enforcement, holding that bringing it into force is for the Central Government. We found no notification bringing it into force up to the date of writing.</p>

<h2>Duties under the Motor Vehicles Act</h2>
<p>Even without Section 106(2), leaving the scene has legal consequences. Section 134 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 requires the driver to:</p>
<ul>
<li>take reasonable steps to get medical help for the injured person, including taking them to the nearest hospital, unless mob anger or another reason beyond the driver's control makes this impossible; and</li>
<li>give the police the information they ask for, or, if no officer is present, report the accident at the nearest police station as soon as possible and in any case within 24 hours.</li>
</ul>
<p>Failing these duties is an offence under Section 187 of the Act. After the 2019 amendment, it carries up to six months, or a fine of Rs 5,000, or both, for a first offence.</p>

<h2>When an accident case becomes Section 105 BNS (culpable homicide)</h2>
<p>Section 106(1) covers death caused by a rash or negligent act "not amounting to culpable homicide". Police sometimes go further and allege that the driver knew the driving was likely to cause death. That is Section 105 BNS (old Section 304 Part II IPC), which is non-bailable and triable by the Sessions Court.</p>
<p>In <em>Alister Anthony Pareira v. State of Maharashtra</em> (2012), a Mumbai drunk-driving case where a car ran over people sleeping on a pavement, the Supreme Court explained the difference. Where rashness or negligence is the cause of death "and nothing more", Section 304A may apply. Where the act is accompanied by knowledge that it is likely to cause death, Section 304 Part II may apply.</p>
<p>In Pune, the Kalyani Nagar Porsche crash of 19 May 2024, in which two people on a motorcycle died, drew wide attention. News reports say the accused was 17. In July 2025 the Juvenile Justice Board refused the police request to try him as an adult, and police then moved the Sessions Court. Those proceedings are still in court, and nothing on this page comments on them.</p>

<h2>Bail, anticipatory bail and police notice</h2>
<p><strong>If only bailable sections are applied</strong> (106(1), 281, 125): bail is a right. Under Section 478 BNSS (old Section 436 CrPC), the police or court must release the person once they furnish bail. Because these offences carry seven years or less, police may also issue a notice to appear under Section 35(3) BNSS (old Section 41A CrPC) instead of arresting. Read more on our <a href="/practice-areas/police-notice-section-35-bnss/">Section 35 BNSS notice page</a>.</p>
<p><strong>If Section 105 BNS is applied</strong>: the offence is non-bailable. Bail is then decided by the court, usually the Sessions Court under Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC), and if refused, the Bombay High Court in Mumbai. There is no Pune bench of the High Court. Our <a href="/bail-after-arrest-pune-guide/">guide to bail after arrest in Pune</a> explains the steps.</p>
<p><strong>Anticipatory bail</strong> under Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC) can be sought from the Pune Sessions Court or the Bombay High Court. It is mainly useful where a non-bailable section such as 105 is applied or feared. See our page on <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Which court hears the case</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Magistrate court:</strong> Sections 106(1), 281 and 125 are triable by a Magistrate. The case runs in the local Magistrate court in Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad, depending on the police station.</li>
<li><strong>Sessions Court:</strong> Section 105 BNS is triable by the Court of Session, at the Pune District and Sessions Court, Shivajinagar. See <a href="/practice-areas/sessions-trial-defence/">Sessions trial defence</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Bombay High Court:</strong> for bail after rejection, quashing petitions and appeals.</li>
</ul>
<p>After investigation, police file a chargesheet. Our page on <a href="/chargesheet-filed-what-next/">what happens after a chargesheet is filed</a> explains the next stages.</p>

<h2>Getting the seized vehicle back (superdari)</h2>
<p>Police usually seize the vehicle. The owner can apply to the Magistrate for interim custody under Section 497 BNSS (old Section 451 CrPC) or Section 503 BNSS (old Section 457 CrPC). This is often called superdari or supurdnama.</p>
<p>In <em>Sunderbhai Ambalal Desai v. State of Gujarat</em> (2002), the Supreme Court said there is no use in keeping seized vehicles at police stations for long. It said Magistrates should pass prompt orders, on a bond and security, after a detailed panchnama and photographs.</p>

<h2>MACT compensation is a separate case</h2>
<p>The victim's family can claim compensation before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal (MACT). This runs separately from the criminal case.</p>
<p>The MACT uses a lower standard of proof. In <em>Mangla Ram v. Oriental Insurance Co.</em> (2018), the Supreme Court held that claims are decided on "preponderance of probabilities", not proof beyond reasonable doubt.</p>

<h2>If the driver was under 18</h2>
<p>A child's case goes to the Juvenile Justice Board, not an ordinary court. Under Section 199A of the Motor Vehicles Act, the guardian or vehicle owner can also be held guilty of the juvenile's motor vehicle offence. This carries up to three years and a fine of Rs 25,000, unless they prove it happened without their knowledge or they took due care. The vehicle's registration is cancelled for 12 months, and the juvenile cannot get a licence until age 25.</p>

<h2>Documents to collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>FIR copy, arrest or notice papers, and the seizure panchnama</li>
<li>Driving licence, RC, insurance policy and PUC certificate</li>
<li>Hospital papers showing the driver helped the injured, if so</li>
<li>Proof that the accident was reported to police, with date and time</li>
<li>Dashcam or CCTV footage, photos of the spot and vehicles, and witness names</li>
<li>Any medical or alcohol test report given to you</li>
</ul>

<h2>Realistic time</h2>
<p>Time for bail, vehicle release, chargesheet and trial varies by court, police station and the sections applied, so no fixed timeline can be given.</p>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Leaving the injured without help or not reporting the accident.</li>
<li>Asking someone else to claim they were driving, or altering the vehicle, CCTV or test samples. This can lead to separate, more serious charges.</li>
<li>Signing statements or settlements with the victim's family without legal advice.</li>
<li>Ignoring a police notice or court date, which can lead to a warrant.</li>
<li>Assuming the MACT claim ends with the criminal case.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Is Section 106 BNS bailable?</h3>
<p>Section 106(1), causing death by negligence, is listed as bailable in the BNSS First Schedule. Section 106(2), the hit-and-run clause, is listed as non-bailable, but it has not been brought into force.</p>
<h3>304A IPC me bail milti hai kya? Is it the same as 106 BNS?</h3>
<p>Yes. Section 106(1) BNS replaced Section 304A IPC for incidents from 1 July 2024. Both are bailable, but the maximum rose from two years to five.</p>
<h3>Is the new 10-year hit-and-run law in force in 2026?</h3>
<p>No notification bringing Section 106(2) into force had been issued at the time of writing. This can change by a government notification, so check the current position.</p>
<h3>अपघात गुन्ह्यात जामीन मिळतो का? (Is bail available in an accident case?)</h3>
<p>For Sections 106(1), 281 and 125 BNS, bail is a right because they are bailable. If police apply Section 105 BNS, bail must be sought from the court, usually the Sessions Court.</p>
<h3>Can I get anticipatory bail in a hit-and-run case in Pune?</h3>
<p>Yes, under Section 482 BNSS from the Pune Sessions Court or the Bombay High Court. It matters most where a non-bailable section like Section 105 is applied or feared.</p>
<h3>How do I get my car released from the police station after an accident?</h3>
<p>Apply to the Magistrate for interim custody under Section 497 or 503 BNSS. The court may release it on bond, with conditions.</p>
<h3>Will the criminal case decide the compensation?</h3>
<p>No. Compensation is decided by the MACT in a separate claim, on a lower standard of proof. Fees for criminal matters depend on court and stage; see our <a href="/criminal-lawyer-fees-pune/">fees page</a>.</p>

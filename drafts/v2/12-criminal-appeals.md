<p><strong>In short:</strong> A person convicted in a criminal case can appeal under <strong>Section 415 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)</strong>, old Section 374 CrPC. A conviction by a Sessions Judge is appealed to the Bombay High Court in Mumbai. A conviction by a Magistrate is appealed to the Sessions Court. The appeal period is 60 days for the High Court and 30 days for the Sessions Court. While the appeal is pending, the court can suspend the sentence and grant bail under <strong>Section 430 BNSS (old Section 389 CrPC)</strong>.</p>

<h2>Appeal under CrPC or BNSS: which section applies</h2>
<p>The CrPC was replaced by the BNSS on 1 July 2024. An offence committed before that date is still tried under the old laws. For the appeal itself, one High Court has held that an appeal filed on or after 1 July 2024 follows the BNSS procedure, even if the trial was under the CrPC. Pending matters stay under the CrPC. Courts may differ, so we cite both numbers.</p>

<h2>Appeal against conviction: Sessions Court or High Court</h2>
<p>Section 415 BNSS decides the forum by who convicted:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Conviction by a Sessions Judge or Additional Sessions Judge:</strong> appeal to the Bombay High Court. The same applies to a conviction by any other court where a sentence of more than seven years was passed on the appellant or a co-accused.</li>
<li><strong>Conviction by a Magistrate:</strong> appeal to the Sessions Court. For Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad cases, this is the Pune District and Sessions Court at Shivajinagar.</li>
</ul>
<p>The Bombay High Court sits in Mumbai. <strong>There is no Pune bench</strong>, so a High Court appeal from a Pune Sessions Court is filed in Mumbai. For Mumbai readers, matters before the Bombay High Court and Mumbai courts are handled, with consultation by phone or video. The office is in Shivajinagar, Pune.</p>
<p>No appeal lies in some situations, such as where the accused pleaded guilty (Section 416 BNSS) or in petty cases (Section 417). A <strong>revision</strong> may still be possible; see below.</p>

<p>A court can condone delay only for sufficient cause, and it is never safe to rely on that. Time spent getting the certified copy of the judgment is generally allowed, but obtain it at once. If you are in jail, a petition can be sent through the jail authorities (Section 424 BNSS, old Section 383 CrPC).</p>

<h2>Bail pending appeal: suspension of sentence under Section 430 BNSS</h2>
<p>Suspension of sentence means the appellate court stops the sentence from running and releases the appellant on bail until the appeal is decided. The conviction remains. After conviction the presumption of innocence no longer works as it does before trial, so courts look at different things from a normal bail application.</p>
<ul>
<li>The court must <strong>record reasons in writing</strong> (Section 430(1)).</li>
<li>Where the offence is punishable with death, life imprisonment or ten years or more, the <strong>Public Prosecutor must first be given a chance to oppose</strong> in writing.</li>
<li>If the sentence is <strong>three years or less</strong> and the person was on bail during trial, the convicting court itself must ordinarily grant bail for long enough to file the appeal, unless it records special reasons (Section 430(3)).</li>
<li>The High Court has the same power for appeals from courts below it (Section 430(2)).</li>
</ul>
<p>Courts commonly look at the length of the sentence and time already served, whether the appeal raises arguable points, conduct on bail during trial, and whether the appeal can be heard before the sentence is served. The Supreme Court's approach differs by sentence. In <em>Aasif @ Pasha</em> (2025 INSC 944), it recalled that for fixed-term sentences, suspension should ordinarily be considered unless exceptional circumstances exist, and set aside a High Court refusal. In <em>Rajesh Upadhyay</em> (2025 INSC 1468), it held that for murder convictions with life sentences, suspension is limited to exceptional cases, and the appellate court should not re-examine the evidence at that stage.</p>
<p>The application is usually filed with the appeal and listed on notice to the State. For general bail law, see <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">bail applications</a>.</p>

<h2>How a High Court appeal moves</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Certified copies</strong> of the judgment, order on sentence and trial record are obtained.</li>
<li><strong>Grounds of appeal</strong> are drafted from the evidence actually recorded.</li>
<li>The appeal and the <strong>suspension application</strong> are filed together in the Bombay High Court, Mumbai.</li>
<li>The suspension application is argued first. Admission of the appeal follows, and final hearing comes later on the full record.</li>
<li>If the sentence is suspended, release is on bail with conditions.</li>
</ol>
<p>For cheque-bounce convictions in a Magistrate's court, the Sessions Court may order a deposit of at least 20% of the fine or compensation under Section 148 of the Negotiable Instruments Act. See <a href="/practice-areas/cheque-bounce-ni-act/">cheque bounce (Section 138)</a>.</p>

<h2>Criminal revision (Sections 438 to 442 BNSS, old 397 to 401 CrPC)</h2>
<p>A revision is not a second appeal. The High Court or the Sessions Judge can call for the record of a lower court to check that a finding, sentence or order is correct, legal and proper. It is used for legal errors, not to re-weigh evidence.</p>
<ul>
<li>It <strong>cannot be used against interlocutory orders</strong> (Section 438(2)).</li>
<li>A person who applies to the Sessions Judge cannot then apply to the High Court on the same matter, and the reverse (Section 438(3)).</li>
<li>Generally, a revision cannot turn an acquittal into a conviction, and it is not available where an appeal lies and was not filed.</li>
<li>Time: 90 days from the order.</li>
</ul>
<p>Orders on discharge or charge are often challenged this way. See <a href="/practice-areas/discharge-application/">discharge application</a> and <a href="/practice-areas/sessions-trial-defence/">sessions trial defence</a>.</p>

<h2>Appeal against acquittal: the State and the victim</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>State.</strong> For a Magistrate's acquittal in a cognizable, non-bailable offence, the District Magistrate may direct the Public Prosecutor to appeal to the Sessions Court. For other acquittals, the State Government may direct an appeal to the High Court, which needs the High Court's leave (Section 419 BNSS).</li>
<li><strong>Complainant.</strong> In a complaint case, the complainant may apply to the High Court for special leave to appeal (Section 419(4) BNSS).</li>
<li><strong>Victim.</strong> The proviso to Section 413 BNSS gives a victim a right to appeal against an acquittal, a conviction for a lesser offence, or inadequate compensation. In <em>Subhas Chandra Sarkar</em> (2026 INSC 1052), the Supreme Court held that a victim's appeal against a Magistrate's acquittal goes to the Sessions Court.</li>
</ul>
<p>For a Section 138 complainant, the Supreme Court held in <em>Celestium Financial v. A. Gnanasekaran</em> (2025) that the complainant is a victim. A February 2026 order (<em>Everest Automobiles</em>) doubted this and referred it to a larger bench. An acquitted accused should have an advocate from the first notice, because the higher court can hear the appeal even years later. After an appeal is notified, the court may require a bail bond for appearance (Section 481 BNSS).</p>

<h2>Supreme Court: special leave petition</h2>
<p>A High Court judgment can be taken to the Supreme Court by a special leave petition (SLP) under Article 136 of the Constitution. Where the High Court reverses an acquittal and sentences a person to death, life imprisonment or ten years or more, the Supreme Court appeal is a statutory right (Section 420 BNSS, old Section 379 CrPC). The SLP period is 90 days in most cases.</p>

<h2>Documents to bring</h2>
<ul>
<li>Judgment and order on sentence</li>
<li>Charge-sheet and, if available, the witnesses' depositions and exhibits</li>
<li>Custody details: date of arrest, periods on bail, date of conviction</li>
<li>Earlier bail orders and the date the certified copy was applied for</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Missing the 30, 60 or 90-day limit while waiting for the certified copy.</li>
<li>Filing a revision where an appeal lies, or the reverse.</li>
<li>Filing a revision in the Sessions Court and again in the High Court.</li>
<li>Asking for suspension without custody details and the points that make the appeal arguable.</li>
<li>Ignoring a State appeal against an acquittal, or a victim's appeal.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Appeal under CrPC Section 374: which section is it now?</h3>
<p>It is Section 415 BNSS. Old Section 374 CrPC covered the same appeals from convictions. An offence before 1 July 2024 is still tried under the old law, but an appeal filed after that date is generally treated under the BNSS.</p>

<h3>Sazaa ke khilaf appeal kitne din mein karni hoti hai?</h3>
<p>Under the Limitation Act, an appeal against conviction to the High Court is 60 days from the sentence. To the Sessions Court it is 30 days. Late appeals need an application showing sufficient cause, so consult an advocate quickly.</p>

<h3>Which court hears an appeal against conviction in a Pune case?</h3>
<p>If a Magistrate convicted you, the appeal goes to the Pune District and Sessions Court. If a Sessions Judge convicted you, it goes to the Bombay High Court in Mumbai, since there is no Pune bench.</p>

<h3>Can I get bail after conviction?</h3>
<p>You can apply for suspension of sentence and bail in the appeal (Section 430 BNSS, old Section 389 CrPC). The court must give reasons. For serious offences it first hears the Public Prosecutor, and for murder convictions with life sentences the Supreme Court has said suspension is only for exceptional cases.</p>

<h3>What is the difference between a criminal appeal and a revision?</h3>
<p>An appeal examines the conviction on the record and can look at the evidence. A revision checks the legality and propriety of an order and is narrower. It does not lie against interlocutory orders, and the 90-day period applies.</p>

<h3>Can the State appeal an acquittal?</h3>
<p>Yes. The State can appeal through the Public Prosecutor, to the Sessions Court or the High Court depending on the case (Section 419 BNSS). A victim can also appeal under the proviso to Section 413, and a complainant can seek special leave.</p>

<h3>Is a criminal appeal in the High Court decided quickly?</h3>
<p>Not always. Final hearing depends on the court's board and the size of the record. This is why the suspension application is filed with the appeal.</p>

<h3>Can I go to the Supreme Court after the High Court decides?</h3>
<p>Yes, by a special leave petition under Article 136, usually within 90 days. In the cases covered by Section 420 BNSS, the appeal is a right.</p>

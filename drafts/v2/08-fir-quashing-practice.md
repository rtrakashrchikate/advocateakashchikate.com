<p><strong>In short:</strong> Quashing of an FIR means asking the Bombay High Court to cancel the FIR and the case that follows from it. The petition is filed under Section 528 BNSS (old Section 482 CrPC) and, often, Article 226 of the Constitution. It is not routine. The High Court allows it only where the FIR discloses no offence, the case is plainly an abuse of process, or a private dispute has genuinely been settled.</p>

<h2>What quashing of an FIR means</h2>
<p>An FIR starts a police investigation. If the police find enough material, they file a chargesheet and a trial follows. Quashing asks the High Court to stop the case before that point, or at any later stage before the trial ends.</p>
<p>People also search for this as a "quash petition" or "FIR quashing petition". It is the same thing: a petition filed in the High Court asking it to set aside an FIR or a pending criminal case. A step-by-step explanation is in our <a href="/fir-quashing-bombay-high-court-pune/">guide to the quash petition, its grounds, documents and procedure</a>.</p>
<p>The High Court does not decide guilt in a quashing petition. It asks a narrower question: should this case be allowed to continue at all?</p>

<h2>Which court can quash an FIR: Bombay High Court at Mumbai</h2>
<p>Only the High Court has this power. A Magistrate or a Sessions Court cannot quash an FIR. Section 528 BNSS speaks of "the inherent powers of the High Court".</p>
<p>There is no Pune bench of the Bombay High Court. A quashing petition against an FIR registered in Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad is heard at the Bombay High Court in Mumbai. The same court hears petitions against FIRs registered in Mumbai.</p>

<h2>Section 528 BNSS (old Section 482 CrPC) and Article 226</h2>
<p>On 1 July 2024 the BNSS replaced the CrPC. Section 482 CrPC became <strong>Section 528 BNSS</strong>. The wording is the same: nothing in the Sanhita limits the High Court's inherent powers to make orders needed to give effect to any order under it, to prevent abuse of the process of any court, or to secure the ends of justice.</p>
<p>The section does not give the High Court new powers. It saves powers the court already has. The Supreme Court has also said the High Court does not act as an appeal or revision court when it uses them.</p>
<p>Petitions are often filed under both Section 528 BNSS and Article 226. FIRs registered before 1 July 2024 are still investigated under the CrPC, so petitions about them may still cite Section 482 CrPC. The legal tests are the same. Our <a href="/section-482-crpc-section-528-bnss-quashing-fir-bombay-high-court/">guide to Section 482 CrPC and Section 528 BNSS</a> explains the section in detail, and the <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">CrPC to BNSS table</a> lists other changed numbers.</p>

<h2>When FIR quashing is available</h2>
<p>A petition can be filed at different stages. The stage changes what the court can look at.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>After the FIR, during investigation.</strong> The court looks mainly at the FIR itself. The Supreme Court has said courts should not stop an investigation at an early stage unless the FIR discloses no offence at all.</li>
<li><strong>After the chargesheet.</strong> The police report under Section 193 BNSS (old Section 173 CrPC) is also before the court, with the statements and documents. Read our note on <a href="/chargesheet-filed-what-next/">what happens after a chargesheet is filed</a>.</li>
<li><strong>After a settlement.</strong> Where the parties in a private dispute have settled, they can ask the court to quash the case on that basis.</li>
<li><strong>Later in the case.</strong> A petition can still be filed, but the Sessions Court may be the better place to ask for discharge. See <a href="/practice-areas/discharge-application/">discharge application</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Quashing is not a substitute for bail. If arrest is the main worry, <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail</a> is a separate and often faster remedy.</p>

<h2>Grounds for quashing of FIR: the Bhajan Lal categories</h2>
<p>The Supreme Court's decision in <strong>State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992 Supp (1) SCC 335)</strong> lists seven kinds of cases where an FIR or complaint can be quashed. In plain words:</p>
<ol>
<li>The allegations, even if fully accepted as true, do not make out any offence.</li>
<li>The FIR and the material with it do not disclose a cognizable offence that justifies a police investigation, unless a Magistrate has ordered one.</li>
<li>The allegations are not disputed, and the evidence collected in support still does not disclose an offence.</li>
<li>The allegations make out only a non-cognizable offence, and police cannot investigate it without a Magistrate's order.</li>
<li>The allegations are so absurd and improbable that no prudent person could find enough ground to proceed.</li>
<li>A law expressly bars the case, or provides its own effective remedy for the grievance.</li>
<li>The case is plainly malicious, filed to take revenge because of a private grudge.</li>
</ol>
<p>Categories 2 and 4 refer to Sections 156 and 155 CrPC. The BNSS equivalents are Section 175 and Section 174. A petition succeeds only when the facts fit one of these categories. A general statement of innocence does not.</p>
<p>The Supreme Court has also warned that quashing should be exercised sparingly and is the exception, not the rule. In <strong>Neeharika Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra (2021)</strong>, a three-judge bench said the court should not examine whether the allegations in an FIR are reliable or genuine at this stage.</p>

<h2>Quashing after a compromise or settlement</h2>
<p>Many petitions come from matrimonial and money disputes that have been settled. Examples are a case under Section 85 BNS (old Section 498A IPC), or a cheating or breach-of-trust case under Section 318(4) or Section 316(2) BNS (old Sections 420 and 406 IPC) that began as a business dispute.</p>
<p>In <strong>Gian Singh v. State of Punjab (2012)</strong>, the Supreme Court held that the High Court can quash a case on settlement even where the offence is not compoundable under Section 320 CrPC (now Section 359 BNSS). The two powers are different. The court said quashing on settlement is not the same as compounding.</p>
<p>The court drew a line. Serious offences such as murder and rape cannot be settled this way, and nor can offences of moral turpitude under special statutes such as the Prevention of Corruption Act. But offences that are mainly civil in flavour can be quashed. The court named civil, commercial, financial, partnership and matrimonial or family disputes, where the wrong is basically to the victim and both sides have settled.</p>
<p>A settlement is not enough by itself. The court checks that it is genuine, that it covers the dispute, and whether continuing the case would serve any purpose. Each case is judged on its own facts.</p>

<h2>What a quashing petition contains</h2>
<p>The contents are set by the facts of the case, but a petition generally has these parts:</p>
<ul>
<li>The parties, the police station, the FIR number and the offences alleged.</li>
<li>A short statement of facts, taken from the FIR and the documents on record.</li>
<li>The specific ground for quashing, tied to a recognised category or to the settlement.</li>
<li>The orders sought: quashing of the FIR, and of the chargesheet and proceedings where those exist.</li>
<li>Annexures: the documents the petition relies on.</li>
<li>Where relevant, a request for interim relief while the petition is pending.</li>
</ul>
<p>The State of Maharashtra is made a respondent, and so is the complainant in most cases. Both are given notice and a chance to reply before the final hearing.</p>

<h2>Interim protection while the petition is pending</h2>
<p>Many people file a quashing petition hoping the court will also stop their arrest. The Supreme Court has set limits on this.</p>
<p>In Neeharika Infrastructure, the Court said an interim order staying the investigation can be passed only with circumspection and not routinely or mechanically. It said that, normally, while an investigation is in progress and the facts are hazy, the High Court should not pass an order of "no coercive steps". The accused should instead apply for anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC, now <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">Section 482 BNSS</a>.</p>
<p>The Court added that where a High Court does grant interim protection, it must give brief reasons and must make clear what "no coercive steps" means. So interim protection is possible, but it is not automatic, and it is argued separately from the final question of quashing.</p>

<h2>Documents to collect</h2>
<ul>
<li>A certified or clear copy of the FIR, with the police station and crime number.</li>
<li>The chargesheet and its annexures, if filed.</li>
<li>Any police notice under <a href="/practice-areas/police-notice-section-35-bnss/">Section 35 BNSS</a>, summons or warrant.</li>
<li>All court orders so far: bail orders, remand orders, orders on cognisance.</li>
<li>The documents that support your case: agreements, bank records, messages, emails, receipts.</li>
<li>For a settlement: the signed settlement or compromise deed, and affidavits from both sides.</li>
<li>Your identity and address proof.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How long a quashing petition takes</h2>
<p>There is no fixed statutory time for deciding a quashing petition, and we have not found an official figure to quote. It depends on the High Court's cause list, whether the State and the complainant file replies, the stage of the case, and whether the matter is argued in full. Treat any promise of a fixed number of weeks or months with caution.</p>
<p>If the petition is dismissed, the case continues before the trial court. Bail, discharge and trial defence remain open. See <a href="/practice-areas/sessions-trial-defence/">sessions trial defence</a>.</p>

<h2>Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treating quashing as a way to avoid arrest.</strong> The court may refuse interim protection, so arrange for bail separately where arrest is a risk.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring police notices.</strong> A pending petition does not by itself cancel a notice or summons.</li>
<li><strong>Relying on a weak settlement.</strong> An unsigned, partial or pressured settlement may not be accepted, and serious offences cannot be settled away.</li>
<li><strong>Arguing disputed facts.</strong> The court does not hold a mini-trial in a quashing petition. Facts that need evidence belong at trial.</li>
<li><strong>Filing without the papers.</strong> A petition without the FIR, chargesheet and orders is slow to be heard.</li>
<li><strong>Using Article 226 or Section 528 for every grievance.</strong> Where another remedy fits better, such as discharge or revision, the court may send you there.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Can an FIR be quashed in the Bombay High Court?</h3>
<p>Yes, but only in limited situations. The FIR may disclose no offence, the case may be an abuse of process, or the parties may have settled a private dispute. The Supreme Court treats quashing as the exception, so the High Court looks closely at the facts.</p>

<h3>Which court can quash an FIR: Magistrate, Sessions Court or High Court?</h3>
<p>Only the High Court. A Magistrate or Sessions Court cannot quash an FIR. They can, at the right stage, refuse to take cognisance or discharge the accused, which is a different remedy.</p>

<h3>Is there a Pune bench of the Bombay High Court for quashing?</h3>
<p>No. There is no Pune bench. A quashing petition about a Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad FIR is heard by the Bombay High Court in Mumbai.</p>

<h3>What is Section 482 CrPC called now?</h3>
<p>It is Section 528 BNSS, in force since 1 July 2024. The wording is the same. People still search for "Section 482 CrPC" and "s 482 CrPC", and older cases and FIRs registered before July 2024 still use that number.</p>

<h3>Can an FIR be quashed after both sides settle?</h3>
<p>Sometimes. Under Gian Singh, the High Court can quash a case arising from a private civil, commercial or matrimonial dispute after a genuine settlement. It will not do so for serious offences such as murder or rape.</p>

<h3>Will the High Court stop my arrest while the quashing petition is pending?</h3>
<p>Not automatically. In Neeharika Infrastructure the Supreme Court said such orders should not be passed routinely and that the accused should normally seek anticipatory bail. Whether protection is given depends on the facts and the stage.</p>

<h3>Can I file for quashing after the chargesheet is filed?</h3>
<p>Yes. A petition can be filed after the chargesheet, and the court may then also look at the police report and documents. Filing a chargesheet does not by itself end the High Court's power to quash.</p>

<h3>How long does an FIR quashing petition take?</h3>
<p>There is no fixed time. It depends on the High Court's list, the replies from the State and complainant, and the stage of the case. A reliable estimate is possible only after the papers have been reviewed.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> A cheque bounce case in Pune is a criminal complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (NI Act). The payee must send a legal notice within 30 days of the bank's return memo. The drawer then has 15 days to pay. If nothing is paid, the complaint must be filed within one month. The punishment is up to 2 years in prison, a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both, and many cases end in settlement.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce meaning: when a returned cheque becomes a crime</h2>
<p>A cheque "bounces" when the bank returns it unpaid. A bounced cheque is not automatically a crime. Section 138 applies only when all of these are true:</p>
<ul>
<li>The cheque was given to pay a <strong>legally enforceable debt or liability</strong>. A gift or a cheque with no real debt behind it does not qualify.</li>
<li>The bank returned it because the account had <strong>insufficient funds</strong>, or because the amount exceeded the arrangement with the bank.</li>
<li>The cheque was presented to the bank within <strong>six months of its date or within its validity period, whichever is earlier</strong>. Since 1 April 2012, the RBI has fixed the validity of a cheque at three months, so in practice this means three months.</li>
<li>The payee sent a written demand notice in time, and the drawer did not pay within 15 days of receiving it.</li>
</ul>
<p>Not every return reason is the same. A return for "signature differs", "account closed" or "payment stopped" raises its own legal questions. The reason written on the bank's return memo matters, so show the memo to an advocate before deciding what to do.</p>
<p>The drawer is the person who signed the cheque. The payee is the person who received it. This page covers both sides.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce charges: bank charges and court penalties</h2>
<p>People searching "cheque bounce charges" usually mean one of two things.</p>
<p><strong>Bank charges.</strong> Banks charge a fee when a cheque is returned. The amount is <strong>not fixed by law</strong>. Each bank sets it in its own schedule of charges, and it can differ between banks and account types. The RBI has said such charges should be levied only where the customer is at fault. Check your bank's current schedule of charges for the exact amount.</p>
<p><strong>Court penalties.</strong> These are the punishment under Section 138, explained below. They are separate from bank charges and are decided only by a court.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce time limit: the deadlines under Section 138</h2>
<p>The time limits are strict. A late notice or a late complaint can end a case, whatever the merits.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Step</th><th>Time allowed</th><th>Counted from</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Present the cheque to the bank</td><td>Within 3 months of the date on the cheque (validity period)</td><td>Date written on the cheque</td></tr>
<tr><td>Send the legal notice</td><td>Within 30 days</td><td>The date the payee receives information from the bank that the cheque is returned unpaid</td></tr>

<p>The court may take up a late complaint if the complainant shows <strong>sufficient cause</strong> for the delay. This is at the court's discretion, so do not rely on it. When you count days, count conservatively and file before the last day.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce legal notice: what the payee must send</h2>
<p>The notice is the foundation of the case. It should:</p>
<ul>
<li>identify the cheque: number, date, amount, drawer's bank;</li>
<li>state the date and reason of the bank's return memo;</li>
<li>demand payment of the cheque amount within 15 days of receipt;</li>
<li>be sent to the drawer's correct address by a mode that gives proof, such as speed post or registered post, and be kept with the tracking record.</li>
</ul>
<p>If the drawer pays within the 15 days, no offence under Section 138 is made out. If the drawer disputes the debt, a written reply within the same period is advisable, because the reply is often read again at trial.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce jurisdiction: which court hears the case (Section 142)</h2>
<p>Section 142(2) of the NI Act, as amended in 2015, decides the court by the <strong>bank branch</strong>, not by where the drawer lives or where the cheque was handed over.</p>
<ul>
<li>If the cheque was given for collection through the payee's account, the court is the one where the <strong>payee's bank branch (the branch holding the account)</strong> is located. If the cheque is dropped at another branch of that bank, it is treated as delivered to the home branch.</li>
<li>If the cheque is presented for payment otherwise than through an account, the court is the one where the <strong>drawer's bank branch</strong> is located.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before the 2015 amendment, the Supreme Court in <em>Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra</em> (2014) had tied the case to the drawee bank's location. Parliament then amended the law, and Section 142A moved pending complaints to the correct court. In the 2025 decision <em>Jai Balaji Industries Ltd. v. HEG Ltd.</em> (2025 INSC 1362), the Supreme Court held that for an account payee cheque the court at the payee's home branch has jurisdiction.</p>
<p>In practice, a payee whose account is at a Pune branch files before a Magistrate court in Pune. A payee with a branch in Pimpri-Chinchwad files in the court covering that area. An accused who lives elsewhere can therefore be summoned to Pune. For Mumbai readers: if the payee's branch is in Mumbai, the case is heard by a Mumbai Magistrate court, and the Bombay High Court in Mumbai hears revisions and quashing petitions. Matters before Mumbai courts and the Bombay High Court are handled, with consultation by phone or video. The office is in Shivajinagar, Pune.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce case procedure: what happens after the complaint</h2>
<p>This is the usual 138 NI Act procedure, in order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Complaint.</strong> The payee files a written complaint with the cheque, return memo, notice and proof of delivery, and an affidavit.</li>
<li><strong>Summons.</strong> The court takes cognizance and issues summons to the accused.</li>
<li><strong>Appearance and bail.</strong> The accused appears and ordinarily furnishes a bail bond. Do not skip the date: repeated absence can lead to a warrant (see <a href="/non-bailable-warrant-cancellation-pune/">non-bailable warrant cancellation</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Plea.</strong> The accusation is explained. The accused pleads guilty or says the case will be contested. Interim compensation can be ordered at this stage.</li>
<li><strong>Evidence.</strong> The complainant's evidence is led, usually by affidavit, followed by cross-examination.</li>
<li><strong>Accused's statement</strong> (Section 351 BNSS, old Section 313 CrPC), then defence evidence if any.</li>
<li><strong>Arguments and judgment.</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>Under Section 143, these cases are tried <strong>summarily</strong>, using the summary-trial procedure now found in the BNSS (old Sections 262 to 265 CrPC). In a summary trial of a Section 138 case the Magistrate can sentence up to <strong>one year</strong>. If a longer sentence may be needed, or summary trial is otherwise unsuitable, the Magistrate records reasons and follows the regular procedure. The law asks the court to try to finish the trial within six months of the complaint. Actual time varies by court and by how many applications are filed.</p>
<p>The offence is generally treated as bailable and not cognizable, so arrest at the start is not the usual route. Still, the case is a criminal one and should be taken seriously.</p>

<h2>Cheque bounce punishment and penalty under Section 138</h2>
<p>On conviction, the court can impose imprisonment up to <strong>2 years</strong>, or a fine up to <strong>twice the cheque amount</strong>, or both. The court can also direct that the fine, or part of it, be paid to the complainant as compensation (Section 395 BNSS, old Section 357 CrPC). Whether a court imposes jail, a fine or both depends on the facts of the case.</p>

<h2>Interim compensation (Section 143A) and appeal deposit (Section 148)</h2>
<p>Two provisions added in 2018 put money on the table before the case is finally decided.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Section 143A, interim compensation.</strong> The trial court may order the accused to pay the complainant interim compensation of up to <strong>20% of the cheque amount</strong>. It is payable within 60 days of the order, extendable by up to 30 days for sufficient cause. If the accused is later acquitted, the complainant must repay it with interest. In <em>Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand</em> (2024 INSC 205), the Supreme Court held that the power is <strong>discretionary, not mandatory</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Section 148, deposit in appeal.</strong> If a convicted accused appeals, the appellate court may order a deposit of at least <strong>20% of the fine or compensation</strong> awarded by the trial court. This is in addition to any interim compensation already paid. In <em>Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation Ltd.</em> (2023), the Supreme Court said the deposit should ordinarily be ordered, and that not ordering it needs special reasons recorded by the court.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Settlement and compounding (Section 147)</h2>
<p>Under Section 147, every offence under the NI Act can be compounded, meaning settled and closed. This is how many cases end. The accused pays the complainant, and the court closes the case.</p>
<p>In September 2025, in <em>Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar</em> (2025 INSC 1158), the Supreme Court revised the cost guidelines first set in <em>Damodar S. Prabhu v. Sayed Babalal H.</em> (2010) 5 SCC 663. The costs, where the court imposes them, are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Compounding <strong>before the accused's evidence is recorded</strong>: no costs.</li>
<li>After defence evidence but <strong>before the Magistrate's judgment</strong>: 5% of the cheque amount.</li>
<li>Before the <strong>Sessions Court or High Court</strong> in revision or appeal: 7.5%.</li>
<li>Before the <strong>Supreme Court</strong>: 10%.</li>
</ul>
<p>These are guidelines and the court keeps its discretion. The message is that early settlement costs less. Special Lok Adalats for Section 138 cases are also held at the Pune District Court, and a case can be settled there.</p>

<h2>How to escape from a cheque bounce case: lawful options only</h2>
<p>There is no way to "escape" a genuine case by avoiding summons, changing address or hiding. That makes things worse and can lead to a warrant. The lawful options are these.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pay within the 15 days.</strong> If the debt is real, payment after receiving the notice means no offence is made out.</li>
<li><strong>Settle.</strong> Settlement and compounding are available at every stage, and earlier is cheaper.</li>
<li><strong>Defend on the facts or the law.</strong> Section 139 presumes the cheque was issued for a debt, but the presumption can be rebutted (<em>Rangappa v. Sri Mohan</em>, (2010) 11 SCC 441). Grounds that are commonly examined, depending on the facts, include: no legally enforceable debt; the debt was already paid; a defective, late or wrongly addressed notice; a late complaint; the wrong court; a disputed signature; and, for companies, whether a director was in charge of the business (Section 141).</li>
</ul>
<p>An honest assessment of these grounds is the starting point. Whether any of them applies depends entirely on your documents.</p>

<h2>If you received a Section 138 notice or summons</h2>
<ol>
<li>Note the <strong>date you received</strong> the notice. The 15 days run from receipt, not from the date printed on it.</li>
<li>Do not ignore it. If the amount is owed, paying within 15 days ends the matter.</li>
<li>If you dispute the debt, send a <strong>written reply</strong> within the 15 days.</li>
<li>Keep the envelope, tracking record and all payment proofs.</li>
<li>If summons come from court, appear on the date given.</li>
</ol>

<h2>If your cheque has bounced (you are the payee)</h2>
<ol>
<li>Collect the <strong>return memo</strong> from your bank and note the date you got it.</li>
<li>Send the <strong>legal notice within 30 days</strong> of that date, with proof of delivery.</li>
<li>Wait <strong>15 days</strong> from delivery. If unpaid, file the complaint <strong>within one month</strong> after that period ends.</li>
<li>File the complaint in the court where your own bank branch (the branch holding your account) is located.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Documents to keep</h2>
<ul>
<li>The original cheque and the bank's return memo, with the reason for return</li>
<li>The legal notice, postal receipts, tracking report or acknowledgement</li>
<li>Any reply sent or received</li>
<li>Proof of the debt: agreement, invoices, ledger, bank statements, messages acknowledging the amount</li>
<li>Identity and address proof; for a company, the authority letter or board resolution</li>
<li>For the accused: the summons, a copy of the complaint and records of payments already made</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Sending the notice after the 30-day period, or counting it from the wrong date.</li>
<li>Sending the notice to an old address, with no proof of delivery.</li>
<li>Filing the complaint after the one-month period, or in the wrong court.</li>
<li>Presenting the cheque after its validity has ended.</li>
<li>Ignoring the notice or the summons, assuming nothing will happen.</li>
<li>Making cash payments without a receipt or written acknowledgement.</li>
</ul>

<h2>After the trial court: appeal, revision and the Bombay High Court</h2>
<p>An accused convicted by a Magistrate can appeal to the Sessions Court (Section 415(3) BNSS, old Section 374(3) CrPC), and can ask for the sentence to be suspended while the appeal is pending. See our page on <a href="/practice-areas/criminal-appeals-suspension-of-sentence/">criminal appeals and suspension of sentence</a>. A complainant whose case ends in acquittal has an appeal route, but the Supreme Court is currently examining whether it needs the High Court's special leave. A February 2026 order (<em>Everest Automobiles</em>) referred this question to a larger bench after doubting the 2025 decision in <em>Celestium Financial v. A. Gnanasekaran</em>. A complainant should check the current position before filing.</p>
<p>In limited situations a petition can also be filed in the Bombay High Court, for example to challenge a legally flawed order (see <a href="/fir-quashing-bombay-high-court-pune/">quashing in the Bombay High Court</a> and <a href="/bombay-high-court-lawyer-pune/">Bombay High Court matters</a>). The right remedy depends on the stage and on the order being challenged.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Cheque bounce hone par kya karna chahiye?</h3>
<p>If you are the payee, collect the bank's return memo and send a legal notice within 30 days. If the drawer does not pay within 15 days, you can file a complaint within one month. If you are the drawer, note the date of receipt of the notice and consider paying or replying in writing within 15 days.</p>

<h3>What is the punishment for cheque bounce under Section 138?</h3>
<p>Up to 2 years in prison, a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both. The court can direct that the fine be paid to the complainant as compensation. Many cases are settled before a sentence is passed.</p>

<h3>How can I get out of a cheque bounce case legally?</h3>
<p>The lawful routes are paying within the 15-day notice period, settling with the complainant at any stage, or defending the case on the facts and the law. Avoiding summons is not a route. It can lead to a warrant and weakens your position.</p>

<h3>What are cheque bounce charges?</h3>
<p>Bank charges for a returned cheque are set by each bank and are not fixed by law, so check your bank's schedule of charges. Separately, the court penalty for the offence is the Section 138 punishment of imprisonment, fine, or both.</p>

<h3>What is the time limit for a cheque bounce case?</h3>
<p>Present the cheque within its validity, send the notice within 30 days of the return information, allow 15 days to pay, and file the complaint within one month after that. The court may excuse a short delay only if sufficient cause is shown.</p>

<h3>Can a cheque bounce case be filed in Pune if the accused lives elsewhere?</h3>
<p>Yes, if the payee's bank branch (the branch holding the payee's account) is in Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad. Under Section 142(2), the court is decided by the bank branch, not by the drawer's address.</p>

<h3>Is there a new law for cheque bounce?</h3>
<p>Section 138 was not changed by the BNSS in July 2024. Recent changes are the 2015 jurisdiction amendment, the 2018 additions of interim compensation (Section 143A) and appeal deposit (Section 148), and the Supreme Court's September 2025 compounding guidelines in <em>Sanjabij Tari</em>.</p>

<h3>Can a cheque bounce case be settled after the complaint is filed?</h3>
<p>Yes. Section 147 makes the offence compoundable, so the parties can settle at any stage, including in a Lok Adalat. Under the 2025 guidelines, settling before the accused's evidence is recorded can be allowed without costs.</p>

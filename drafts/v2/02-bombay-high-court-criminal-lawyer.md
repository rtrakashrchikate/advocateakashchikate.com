<h2>Which matters go to the Bombay High Court</h2>

<h3>Bail in the Bombay High Court</h3>
<p>Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC) gives the High Court and the Sessions Court special powers to grant bail to a person in custody. Bail is usually sought first from the Magistrate or Sessions Court. If the Sessions Court refuses, the next step is a bail application before the High Court.</p>
<p>The application deals with the reasons the lower court gave. It sets out the facts, how long the accused has been in custody, the stage of the investigation or trial, and any new material. Special laws can add conditions: for example, Section 37 of the NDPS Act for drug cases. See <a href="/bail-rejected-sessions-court-pune-what-next/">what to do after a Sessions Court refuses bail</a>, <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">regular bail applications</a>, <a href="/ndps-act-bail-pune/">NDPS bail</a> and <a href="/economic-offences-bail-bombay-high-court/">economic offences bail</a>.</p>

<h3>Anticipatory bail</h3>
<p>Anticipatory bail is protection from arrest, sought under Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC). The section names both the High Court and the Court of Session. Many applicants go to the Sessions Court first. If it refuses, a fresh application can be made to the High Court. This is not an appeal against the Sessions Court order, but a new application to a higher court. See <a href="/how-to-get-anticipatory-bail-pune/">how to get anticipatory bail</a> and <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">the anticipatory bail page</a>.</p>

<h3>FIR quashing</h3>
<p>The High Court can quash an FIR or a criminal case under its inherent powers in Section 528 BNSS (old Section 482 CrPC). These powers are used sparingly. In <em>State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal</em>, the Supreme Court listed categories where quashing may be justified. Examples are allegations that, even if taken as true, disclose no offence, allegations that are absurd or inherently improbable, and a case started with an ulterior motive. In Bombay High Court practice, such petitions are filed as a criminal writ petition or a criminal application under Section 528 BNSS. The court also considers cases where the parties have settled. Read about <a href="/practice-areas/fir-quashing/">FIR quashing</a> and <a href="/fir-quashing-bombay-high-court-pune/">FIR quashing in the Bombay High Court</a>.</p>

<h3>Criminal appeals and suspension of sentence</h3>
<p>A person convicted by a Sessions Judge can appeal to the High Court (Section 415 BNSS, old Section 374 CrPC). While the appeal is pending, the High Court can suspend the sentence and release the appellant on bail (Section 430 BNSS, old Section 389 CrPC). An order of a lower court can also be challenged by a criminal revision (Sections 438 and 442 BNSS, old Sections 397 and 401 CrPC). Appeals have short time limits, so take the judgment to a lawyer at once. See <a href="/practice-areas/criminal-appeals-suspension-of-sentence/">criminal appeals and suspension of sentence</a>.</p>

<h3>Writ petitions against detention</h3>
<p>A person held under a preventive detention order, or held illegally, can ask the High Court for relief by a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution. This is often called a habeas corpus petition. In Maharashtra, preventive detention orders come under laws such as the Maharashtra Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act, 1981 (MPDA). The High Court reads the grounds of detention and the procedure followed.</p>

<h2>How a High Court matter moves</h2>
<p>The steps below describe the usual course. The registry and the bench decide details in each case.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Preparation.</strong> The advocate reads the FIR, the lower court orders and the documents. A petition or application is drafted with these papers attached.</li>
<li><strong>Filing.</strong> The petition is filed with the High Court registry in Mumbai. The Bombay High Court uses e-filing for many criminal matters. The registry may point out defects, which must be corrected before the matter is accepted.</li>
<li><strong>Listing.</strong> The matter is given a number and listed before a bench on a date shown in the cause list. In an urgent case, the advocate can ask the court to list it earlier.</li>
<li><strong>Hearing.</strong> The State, through the Public Prosecutor, is heard. The court may issue notice, ask the State for a reply, or pass an interim order. Interim relief can include protection from arrest or a stay of proceedings. The advocate then argues the matter on the law and the documents.</li>
<li><strong>Order.</strong> The court allows, rejects or adjourns the application, and gives reasons. The order is uploaded on the court website. A further challenge can go to the Supreme Court of India.</li>
</ol>
<p>How long each step takes depends on the court's list and on the case. No fixed period can be promised.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Can I apply for bail directly in the Bombay High Court?</h3>
<p>Section 483 BNSS gives the High Court power to grant bail. In practice, regular bail is usually sought first from the Magistrate or Sessions Court. The High Court may ask why the lower court was not approached. Which course suits your case depends on the facts, so ask your advocate.</p>
<h3>The Sessions Court refused anticipatory bail. Can I appeal?</h3>
<p>There is no appeal as such. Section 482 BNSS (old Section 438 CrPC) names both the High Court and the Court of Session, so you can make a fresh application to the High Court. The application should address the reasons the Sessions Court gave.</p>
<h3>Is there a Bombay High Court bench in Pune?</h3>
<p>No. The Bombay High Court sits in Mumbai at its principal seat, and a matter from Pune is heard there. It has benches in some other places, but none at Pune.</p>
<h3>I live in Mumbai. Can I consult a criminal advocate in Pune for my case?</h3>
<p>Yes, for matters before the Bombay High Court and Mumbai courts. The advocate's office is in Pune and there is no Mumbai office. Consultation is by phone or video, documents come by WhatsApp or email, and the advocate appears at the hearing in Mumbai.</p>
<h3>When can an FIR be quashed under Section 528 BNSS?</h3>
<p>The High Court quashes an FIR sparingly. Examples are allegations that disclose no offence even if true, allegations that are absurd, and a complaint made with an ulterior motive. Settlement between the parties is also considered in some cases. Each petition depends on the FIR's wording and the facts.</p>
<h3>I was convicted. Can the sentence be suspended while my appeal is pending?</h3>
<p>The appeal court can suspend the sentence and release the appellant on bail under Section 430 BNSS (old Section 389 CrPC). This is a request the court decides on the facts, including the sentence, the conviction and the time the appeal will take. File the appeal promptly, because appeals have time limits.</p>
<h3>Can a detention order be challenged in the High Court?</h3>
<p>Yes. A writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution can challenge a preventive detention order, such as one under the MPDA Act, or an illegal detention. The petition looks at the grounds and the procedure followed. Details are in the order, so bring a copy.</p>

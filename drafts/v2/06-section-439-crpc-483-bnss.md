<p><strong>In short:</strong> Section 439 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) gave the High Court and the Court of Session special powers over bail. Since 1 July 2024 the same power is in Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS). The two courts can release an accused who is in custody, set conditions, change a Magistrate's conditions, and order a person released on bail to be arrested again. In Pune this means the Sessions Court at Shivajinagar. For the High Court it means the Bombay High Court in Mumbai.</p>

<h2>Section 439 CrPC and Section 483 BNSS: old vs new</h2>
<p>Section 483 BNSS carries forward Section 439 CrPC with the same heading, "Special powers of High Court or Court of Session regarding bail". The structure is close. The changes are mainly cross-references to the new criminal codes. For cases registered before 1 July 2024, a court may still refer to the old CrPC numbers. See the <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">CrPC to BNSS section table</a>.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Point</th><th>Old: CrPC</th><th>New: BNSS</th></tr>
<tr><td>Power to release an accused in custody</td><td>Section 439(1)(a)</td><td>Section 483(1)(a)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Conditions for serious offences</td><td>Refers to Section 437(3)</td><td>Refers to Section 480(3)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Set aside or change a Magistrate's condition</td><td>Section 439(1)(b)</td><td>Section 483(1)(b)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Notice to Public Prosecutor (offence triable only by Sessions Court, or punishable with life imprisonment)</td><td>First proviso to Section 439(1)</td><td>First proviso to Section 483(1)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Notice within 15 days, and informant present, for listed sexual offences</td><td>Second proviso and Section 439(1A) (IPC 376(3), 376AB, 376DA, 376DB)</td><td>Second proviso and Section 483(2) (BNS Section 65 and 70(2))</td></tr>
<tr><td>Arrest of a person already released on bail</td><td>Section 439(2)</td><td>Section 483(3)</td></tr>
</table>

<h2>What the section allows</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Grant bail.</strong> The High Court or Court of Session may direct that a person accused of an offence and in custody be released on bail.</li>
<li><strong>Set conditions.</strong> For offences covered by Section 480(3) BNSS, the court may impose the conditions it considers necessary. Those are offences punishable with seven years or more and certain other listed offences.</li>
<li><strong>Change a Magistrate's conditions.</strong> A condition fixed by a Magistrate can be set aside or modified.</li>
<li><strong>Cancel bail or order re-arrest.</strong> Under Section 483(3), the court may direct that a person released on bail under this Chapter be arrested and committed to custody. This is the old Section 439(2). See <a href="/practice-areas/bail-application/">bail application in Pune</a> for how cancellation fits with the rest of the bail process.</li>
</ul>
<p>The section works after arrest. It is not for protection before arrest. That is Section 482 BNSS, covered below.</p>

<h2>Which court in Pune and Mumbai</h2>
<p><strong>Pune District and Sessions Court, Shivajinagar.</strong> This is the usual forum for a bail application under Section 483 in a serious case registered in Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad. The advocate's office is in Shivajinagar, near this court.</p>
<p><strong>Bombay High Court, Mumbai.</strong> The High Court has the same power under Section 483. There is no Pune bench, so these matters are filed and heard in Mumbai. The office is in Pune, and matters before the Bombay High Court are handled with consultation by phone or video. See <a href="/bombay-high-court-lawyer-pune/">Bombay High Court criminal lawyer</a>.</p>
<p>Both courts have power under the section. In practice, an accused commonly goes to the Sessions Court first, and to the High Court if bail is refused. Whether a direct High Court application is accepted can depend on the case and the bench.</p>

<h2>How Section 483 differs from Sections 480 and 482 BNSS</h2>
<table>
<tr><th>Section</th><th>Old CrPC</th><th>Who decides</th><th>When</th></tr>
<tr><td>480</td><td>437</td><td>Magistrate and courts other than the Sessions Court and High Court</td><td>After arrest, non-bailable offence. Cannot grant bail if there are reasonable grounds to believe the offence is punishable with death or life imprisonment, with limited exceptions.</td></tr>
<tr><td>482</td><td>438</td><td>High Court or Court of Session</td><td>Before arrest, when a person fears arrest for a non-bailable offence. Does not apply to offences under Section 65 and 70(2) BNS.</td></tr>
<tr><td>483</td><td>439</td><td>High Court or Court of Session</td><td>After arrest, when the accused is in custody. Wider than Section 480.</td></tr>
</table>
<p>The Section 480 bar on death and life imprisonment offences does not limit the Sessions Court or High Court. The Supreme Court said this about the old law in <em>Gurcharan Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)</em> (1978) 1 SCC 118. That does not mean bail is easy in such cases. Courts look closely at the facts and at the notice to the Public Prosecutor. For the pre-arrest remedy see <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Key Supreme Court principles</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Concurrent power.</strong> In <em>Gurcharan Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)</em> (1978) 1 SCC 118, the Court held that the High Court can act under the cancellation power even where the Sessions Judge granted bail. The State may go to the Sessions Judge if new circumstances arise, or to the High Court.</li>
<li><strong>Bail is the rule.</strong> In <em>Sanjay Chandra v. CBI</em> (2012) 1 SCC 40, the Court said bail is the rule and jail the exception, and pre-trial detention should not be punishment. The seriousness of the charge and the wider circumstances both count.</li>
<li><strong>Cancelling bail is harder than refusing it.</strong> In <em>Dolat Ram v. State of Haryana</em> (1995) 1 SCC 349, the Court said very cogent and overwhelming circumstances are needed to cancel bail already granted. Refusing bail at the start and cancelling it later rest on different bases.</li>
<li><strong>A perverse bail order can be set aside.</strong> In <em>Deepak Yadav v. State of U.P.</em> (2022) 8 SCC 559, the Court held that cancellation is not limited to misconduct after release. An order that is perverse or ignores relevant material can also be set aside.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What to expect at a hearing</h2>
<p>The application sets out the FIR, the sections, the date of arrest, any earlier bail order and the grounds. The Public Prosecutor is heard. Courts commonly weigh the gravity of the offence, the risk of tampering or flight, past cases, the stage of investigation and the time already in custody. If bail is granted, the accused signs a bond and gives a surety. See <a href="/bail-after-arrest-pune-guide/">bail after arrest in Pune</a>. If the Sessions Court refuses, read <a href="/bail-rejected-sessions-court-pune-what-next/">bail rejected: what next</a>.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>What is Section 439 CrPC?</h3>
<p>It was the CrPC provision giving the High Court and Court of Session special powers on bail. They could grant bail to an accused in custody, change conditions and order re-arrest. It is now Section 483 BNSS.</p>
<h3>What is Section 439 CrPC in the new law?</h3>
<p>Section 483 BNSS. The wording is largely the same. The sexual-offence provisos now refer to Sections 65 and 70(2) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita instead of the old IPC sections.</p>
<h3>Which court hears a Section 483 BNSS application in Pune?</h3>
<p>The Pune District and Sessions Court at Shivajinagar, and the Bombay High Court in Mumbai. The High Court has no bench in Pune.</p>
<h3>Can the Sessions Court grant bail in a case punishable with life imprisonment?</h3>
<p>Yes. The Section 480 bar that applies to Magistrates does not bind the Sessions Court or High Court. The court must first notify the Public Prosecutor, unless it records that notice is not practicable.</p>
<h3>Can bail granted under Section 439 or 483 be cancelled?</h3>
<p>Yes, under Section 483(3) BNSS (old Section 439(2) CrPC). Courts cancel for reasons such as tampering with witnesses, breach of conditions or a perverse bail order. They treat cancellation more strictly than refusing bail.</p>
<h3>How is Section 439 CrPC different from Section 438 CrPC?</h3>
<p>Section 438 (now Section 482 BNSS) is anticipatory bail, sought before arrest. Section 439 (now Section 483) is for a person already in custody.</p>
<h3>Can a Magistrate use Section 483 BNSS?</h3>
<p>No. A Magistrate uses Section 480 BNSS (old Section 437 CrPC). Section 483 is only for the High Court and the Court of Session.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> A bail application asks a court to release a person who is already in custody, on a bond and usually with a surety. A Magistrate decides it under Section 480 BNSS (old Section 437 CrPC). The Sessions Court at Shivajinagar and the Bombay High Court decide it under Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC). If the offence is bailable, release is a matter of right. If it is non-bailable, the court weighs the facts.</p>

<p>This page is for families in Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad whose relative has been arrested. Anticipatory bail (before arrest) is a different remedy: see <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">anticipatory bail in Pune</a>. The office is in Shivajinagar, Pune, near the Pune District and Sessions Court. Matters before the Bombay High Court in Mumbai and Mumbai courts are also handled, with consultation by phone or video.</p>

<h2>Bailable and non-bailable offences</h2>
<p>Everything starts with one question: is the offence bailable or non-bailable? The First Schedule to the BNSS lists this for each offence (Section 2(1) BNSS defines both terms). Your FIR copy shows the sections applied.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bailable offence:</strong> Section 478 BNSS (old Section 436 CrPC) says the person must be released on bail if ready to furnish it. The police officer or the court cannot refuse. If a person cannot give a surety, the court may release an indigent person on a personal bond, and may presume indigence if bail is not furnished within one week of arrest.</li>
<li><strong>Non-bailable offence:</strong> bail is not a right. The court uses its discretion and records reasons. This is where most bail applications, and most of the work, lie.</li>
</ul>
<p>Section 479 BNSS (old Section 436A CrPC) separately limits how long an undertrial can stay in jail. It does not apply to offences punishable with death or life imprisonment. See <a href="/practice-areas/default-bail-undertrial-release/">default bail and undertrial release</a>.</p>

<p>There is no Pune bench of the Bombay High Court. High Court bail matters are filed and heard in Mumbai.</p>
<p>For cases registered before 1 July 2024, a court may still refer to the old CrPC numbers. The test for bail is the same, and the sections only renumbered. See the <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">CrPC to BNSS section table</a>.</p>

<h2>Bail after arrest and remand: the first days</h2>
<p>Under Section 58 BNSS, the police must produce an arrested person before a Magistrate within 24 hours, excluding travel time. The Magistrate then decides remand under Section 187 BNSS (old Section 167 CrPC). The first remand hearing is itself a chance to apply for bail.</p>
<ul>
<li>Police custody can be up to 15 days in total. Under Section 187 BNSS, those days can be taken in parts within the first 40 days (of a 60-day period) or 60 days (of a 90-day period).</li>
<li>Section 480(1) BNSS says the need for identification, or for police custody beyond the first 15 days, is not by itself a reason to refuse bail if the accused agrees to follow the court's directions.</li>
<li>If the chargesheet is not filed within 60 days (90 days for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment or ten years or more), the accused has a right to default bail, if prepared to furnish bail.</li>
</ul>
<p>Read more: <a href="/practice-areas/police-custody-remand/">police custody and remand in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Section 480 BNSS (old Section 437 CrPC): the Magistrate's power</h2>
<p>A Magistrate can grant bail in a non-bailable offence, but Section 480(1) sets limits. Bail is barred where there are reasonable grounds to believe the offence is punishable with death or life imprisonment. The court may still release a child, a woman, or a sick or infirm person. A prior conviction record can also bar bail in cognizable offences. Before granting bail in offences punishable with seven years or more, the Magistrate must hear the Public Prosecutor.</p>
<p>The Magistrate must record reasons for granting bail (Section 480(4)). Under Section 480(6), if a Magistrate's trial is not finished within 60 days from the first date fixed for evidence and the accused has been in custody throughout, the accused should be released on bail, unless the Magistrate records reasons in writing.</p>

<h2>Section 483 BNSS (old Section 439 CrPC): Sessions Court and Bombay High Court</h2>
<p>Section 483(1) lets the High Court and the Court of Session direct that an accused in custody be released on bail, and set aside or change a condition imposed by a Magistrate. The Section 480 bars on death and life offences do not limit these courts. The Supreme Court said so in <em>Gurcharan Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)</em> (1978) 1 SCC 118. In practice, for offences triable only by the Sessions Court, the Sessions Court is the first stop.</p>
<p>Before granting bail in an offence triable only by the Court of Session, or one punishable with life imprisonment, the court must notify the Public Prosecutor, unless it records that notice is not practicable.</p>

<h2>What courts weigh in a non-bailable case</h2>
<p>There is no fixed formula. The Supreme Court has said that bail is the rule and jail the exception, that pre-trial detention should not be punishment, and that both the seriousness of the charge and the wider circumstances count (<em>Sanjay Chandra v. CBI</em>, (2012) 1 SCC 40). Courts commonly look at:</p>
<ul>
<li>the nature and gravity of the allegation and the punishment it carries;</li>
<li>whether the accused may flee, or has roots in Pune and a fixed address;</li>
<li>whether the accused may tamper with evidence or influence witnesses;</li>
<li>any earlier cases or convictions;</li>
<li>how far the investigation has come, and whether custody is still needed;</li>
<li>the time already spent in custody and the likely time for trial.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Bail conditions</h2>
<p>For offences punishable with seven years or more, and some other listed offences, Section 480(3) BNSS makes certain conditions compulsory: attend as the bond requires, do not commit a similar offence, do not threaten or induce witnesses, and do not tamper with evidence. The court may add other conditions it thinks necessary. Conditions depend on the case. A Magistrate's conditions can be challenged before the Sessions Court or High Court under Section 483(1)(b). Breaking a condition is a ground to seek cancellation.</p>

<h2>Surety and bonds</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bond and surety:</strong> before release, the person signs a bond for an amount the court considers adequate. If released on a bail bond, one or more suitable sureties are needed (Section 485 BNSS). Any bail condition must be written into the bond.</li>
<li><strong>Amount:</strong> under Section 484 BNSS the bond amount must have regard to the circumstances and must not be excessive. The High Court or Sessions Court can reduce the amount fixed by a police officer or Magistrate.</li>
<li><strong>Fitness of surety:</strong> the court may accept affidavits to decide whether a surety is fit and sufficient, or inquire itself.</li>
<li><strong>If the accused does not appear:</strong> the bond can be forfeited under Section 491 BNSS.</li>
</ul>
<p>The papers a surety must bring differ from court to court in Pune. Ask the court staff or your advocate before the surety attends. Fees depend on court and stage and are agreed at consultation: see <a href="/criminal-lawyer-fees-pune/">criminal lawyer fees in Pune</a>.</p>

<h2>Cancellation of bail</h2>
<p>Bail can be cancelled after it is granted. The powers are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Section 480(5) BNSS (old Section 437(5) CrPC): the court that granted bail can order the person arrested and committed to custody.</li>
<li>Section 483(3) BNSS (old Section 439(2) CrPC): the High Court or Court of Session can order the same for a person released on bail under this Chapter.</li>
</ul>
<p>The State usually applies. A complainant can also apply. Courts treat cancellation more strictly than refusing bail at the start. In <em>Dolat Ram v. State of Haryana</em> (1995) 1 SCC 349, the Supreme Court said very cogent and overwhelming circumstances are needed to cancel bail already granted. In <em>Deepak Yadav v. State of U.P.</em> (2022) 8 SCC 559, it said cancellation is not limited to later misconduct: a bail order that is perverse or ignores material facts can also be set aside. Typical grounds are tampering with witnesses, breach of conditions, absconding or a fresh offence. The accused is heard before a decision.</p>

<h2>Bail application draft: what it contains</h2>
<p>Each court has its own form and numbering for a bail application, sometimes called a criminal miscellaneous bail application. A bail application usually includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>the court, case title and the accused's details;</li>
<li>the FIR number, police station, date, sections applied and date of arrest;</li>
<li>a short statement of facts and the grounds for bail, one by one;</li>
<li>whether an earlier bail application was filed or rejected, and with what result;</li>
<li>the prayer, with an offer to follow conditions.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Documents to collect</h3>
<ul>
<li>copy of the FIR and arrest memo or intimation;</li>
<li>remand order and any earlier bail order;</li>
<li>chargesheet or case papers, if filed;</li>
<li>identity and address proof of the accused and the proposed surety;</li>
<li>medical papers, if health is a ground;</li>
<li>proof of employment, family responsibility or settled residence in Pune.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Bail rejected: the next step</h2>
<p>A rejection by the Magistrate can be taken to the Sessions Court. A rejection by the Sessions Court can be taken to the Bombay High Court under Section 483 BNSS. A fresh application in the same court usually needs a real change in circumstances, such as a filed chargesheet or longer custody. Read <a href="/bail-rejected-sessions-court-pune-what-next/">bail rejected by the Sessions Court in Pune: what next</a> and <a href="/bombay-high-court-lawyer-pune/">Bombay High Court criminal matters</a>.</p>
<p>How long a court takes depends on the court and its list. The Supreme Court has directed that bail applications should be decided within two weeks unless the law requires otherwise (<em>Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI</em>, (2022) 10 SCC 51), but this is a guideline, not a fixed date. To track a High Court hearing, check the daily cause list on the court's official website.</p>

<h2>Mistakes to avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li>Filing in the wrong court, or using anticipatory bail after arrest has already happened.</li>
<li>Giving a general plea with no dates, documents or address proof.</li>
<li>Hiding an earlier rejected application. Courts expect it to be disclosed.</li>
<li>Breaking a condition, such as not attending the police station or court.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Jamanat kaise milti hai?</h3>
<p>If the offence is bailable, the police or the court must release the person on bail once a bond (and surety if needed) is given. In a non-bailable offence, you apply to the court, which weighs the facts. See <a href="/jamanat-kitne-din-me-milti-hai-kharcha-documents-pune/">jamanat kitne din me milti hai</a>.</p>
<h3>What is the difference between Section 437 and Section 439 CrPC?</h3>
<p>Section 437 CrPC (now Section 480 BNSS) is the Magistrate's power. Section 439 CrPC (now Section 483 BNSS) belongs to the Sessions Court and High Court, and is wider. Both are explained above.</p>
<h3>Which court should file a bail application in Pune?</h3>
<p>For bailable offences, the police or Magistrate. For most non-bailable offences, the Magistrate or the Sessions Court at Shivajinagar, depending on the offence. The Bombay High Court is approached after a Sessions Court refusal or where the law allows.</p>
<h3>Can the court cancel my bail?</h3>
<p>Yes, under Section 480(5) or 483(3) BNSS (old Section 437(5) and 439(2) CrPC). Courts do this for tampering, breach of conditions, absconding or a perverse bail order. They are cautious, and you are heard first.</p>
<h3>My bail was rejected. What can I do?</h3>
<p>Move the higher court: Sessions Court after a Magistrate, and Bombay High Court after a Sessions Court. A new application in the same court needs changed circumstances. See the guide linked above.</p>
<h3>Does a surety have to be from Pune?</h3>
<p>The law does not fix this in Section 485 BNSS, which asks that sureties be suitable. Courts decide fitness case by case, and practice varies. Ask the court or your advocate before arranging one.</p>
<h3>Where do I see the High Court bail list?</h3>
<p>The Bombay High Court publishes daily cause lists, the list of matters to be heard, on its official website. An advocate can confirm the court number and time for a bail matter.</p>
<h3>Is bail possible if the police need more custody?</h3>
<p>Yes. Under Section 480(1) BNSS, the need for police custody beyond the first 15 days is not by itself a reason to refuse bail, if the accused agrees to follow the court's directions.</p>

<p><strong>In short:</strong> Section 482 CrPC saved the inherent powers of the High Court. Since 1 July 2024 the same power sits in <strong>Section 528 BNSS</strong>, with the same wording. The High Court uses it to quash an FIR or case to prevent abuse of process or secure the ends of justice. It is used sparingly, and it differs from discharge and criminal revision.</p>

<h2>Section 482 CrPC and Section 528 BNSS: old vs new</h2>
<p>The BNSS replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 on 1 July 2024. The inherent-powers section kept its wording. Only the number and the word "Code" (now "Sanhita") changed.</p>
<p>Section 528 BNSS reads: "Nothing in this Sanhita shall be deemed to limit or affect the inherent powers of the High Court to make such orders as may be necessary to give effect to any order under this Sanhita, or to prevent abuse of the process of any Court or otherwise to secure the ends of justice."</p>

<p>Note the trap: Section 482 CrPC is the inherent-powers section, but Section 482 BNSS is anticipatory bail. People searching "Section 482" get both. Our <a href="/crpc-to-bnss-ipc-to-bns-section-table/">CrPC to BNSS table</a> lists more numbers.</p>
<p>Offences before 1 July 2024 are still dealt with under the old laws, and earlier FIRs are still investigated under the CrPC. Petitions about them often use the old number. The tests are the same.</p>

<h2>What are the inherent powers of a court under the CrPC?</h2>
<p>"Inherent" means a power that a court has because it is a court, not because a statute grants it. Section 482 CrPC did not create the High Court's inherent power. It only recognised and saved it. The Supreme Court has said so, and Section 528 BNSS works the same way.</p>
<p>The Supreme Court has said that this power:</p>
<ul>
<li>is wide, but must be used sparingly, carefully and with caution;</li>
<li>does not make the High Court an appeal or revision court; and</li>
<li>does not give an arbitrary jurisdiction to act on whim or caprice.</li>
</ul>
<p>It is also not a way around the rest of the Code. The Supreme Court has noted that inherent powers cannot be used for matters that other provisions of the Code specifically cover.</p>

<h2>The three purposes of Section 528 BNSS</h2>
<p>The section lists three purposes. The Supreme Court has described them as three circumstances in which the power may be used:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>To give effect to an order under the Sanhita.</strong> For example, an order needed to make an earlier order work.</li>
<li><strong>To prevent abuse of the process of any court.</strong> Most FIR quashing petitions rely on this purpose. It covers a case brought or continued in a way that misuses the court.</li>
<li><strong>To secure the ends of justice.</strong> This covers cases where letting the case go on would cause injustice, for example after a genuine settlement of a private dispute.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Limits: not a mini-trial</h2>
<p>The main limit is that a quashing petition is not a trial. In <strong>Neeharika Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra (2021)</strong>, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court summarised the position. Among its points:</p>
<ul>
<li>The power to quash should be used sparingly and with circumspection. Quashing should be the exception, not the ordinary rule.</li>
<li>The court cannot enquire into whether the allegations in the FIR are reliable or genuine.</li>
<li>An FIR is not an encyclopaedia. It need not state every fact, and the court should not decide on hazy facts that it does not deserve investigation.</li>
<li>The court should ordinarily not interfere at the stage of investigation, except in exceptional cases where non-interference would cause a miscarriage of justice.</li>
</ul>
<p>At the same time the Court confirmed that the High Court does have jurisdiction to quash an FIR within the parameters in <strong>R.P. Kapur</strong> and <strong>State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992)</strong>. Where the FIR itself discloses no cognizable offence, the investigation does not have to go on.</p>

<h2>When High Courts quash and when they refuse</h2>
<p>The court looks at the FIR, the material before it and the stage of the case. These situations can support quashing:</p>
<ul>
<li>The allegations, taken as true, do not make out any offence.</li>
<li>The allegations are so absurd and improbable that no prudent person could proceed on them.</li>
<li>A law expressly bars the case, or the case is plainly malicious.</li>
<li>A private civil, commercial or matrimonial dispute has been genuinely settled. This comes from <strong>Gian Singh v. State of Punjab (2012)</strong>, where the Supreme Court said quashing on settlement is different from compounding under Section 320 CrPC (now Section 359 BNSS).</li>
</ul>
<p>The court is likely to refuse where:</p>
<ul>
<li>the FIR discloses a cognizable offence and the facts are disputed and need evidence;</li>
<li>the investigation is at an early stage and the material is not before the court;</li>
<li>the offence is serious, such as murder or rape, and the only ground is a private settlement; or</li>
<li>the petitioner is really asking the court to weigh evidence, as a trial court would.</li>
</ul>

<p>On revision, the High Court or the Sessions Judge may call for the record of an inferior criminal court to check the correctness, legality or propriety of a finding, sentence or order. The High Court's wider revision powers are in Section 442 BNSS (old 401 CrPC).</p>
<p>The remedies are not interchangeable, and a quashing petition is not a second appeal. For discharge, see <a href="/practice-areas/discharge-application/">discharge application</a>. For the trial stage, see <a href="/practice-areas/sessions-trial-defence/">sessions trial defence</a>.</p>

<h2>Maharashtra and Bombay High Court</h2>
<p>In Maharashtra, quashing petitions go to the Bombay High Court at Mumbai. There is no Pune bench, so a petition about a Pune or Pimpri-Chinchwad FIR is heard in Mumbai.</p>
<p>The main Supreme Court case on interim orders in quashing petitions, Neeharika Infrastructure, itself came from Maharashtra. It arose from an FIR at Worli Police Station, Mumbai, and a Bombay High Court order of "no coercive steps" against the accused. The Supreme Court set that order aside and said such orders should not be routine. Normally, it said, the accused should apply for anticipatory bail, now <a href="/practice-areas/anticipatory-bail/">Section 482 BNSS</a>.</p>
<p>In a September 2025 order from a Bombay High Court petition, the Supreme Court said a chargesheet does not by itself make a Section 528 BNSS petition pointless. Once a Magistrate has taken cognisance, Article 226 is no longer available, but Section 528 can reach the FIR, chargesheet and cognisance order.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>What is Section 482 CrPC in simple words?</h3>
<p>It saves the High Court's inherent power to make orders that give effect to an order, prevent abuse of process, or secure the ends of justice. In practice it is the main source of the power to quash an FIR. Since 1 July 2024 it is Section 528 BNSS.</p>

<h3>Is Section 528 BNSS the same as Section 482 CrPC?</h3>
<p>Yes, in substance. Section 528 BNSS repeats Section 482 CrPC with the word "Sanhita" in place of "Code". The case law on Section 482 CrPC, such as Bhajan Lal and Gian Singh, is still used for Section 528.</p>

<h3>Is Section 482 BNSS the inherent-powers section?</h3>
<p>No. Section 482 BNSS is anticipatory bail, the old Section 438 CrPC. The inherent-powers section is Section 528 BNSS. The numbers moved around in the change, so check the section heading.</p>

<h3>Can a Magistrate or Sessions Court use inherent powers to quash an FIR?</h3>
<p>No. Section 528 BNSS speaks of the inherent powers of the High Court. A Magistrate or Sessions Court can discharge the accused at the right stage, but it cannot quash an FIR.</p>

<h3>Can the High Court quash an FIR where the facts are disputed?</h3>
<p>Usually not. The court does not weigh evidence or decide whose version is true in a quashing petition. If the dispute can be resolved only by evidence, the matter generally goes to trial.</p>

<h3>What is the difference between quashing and criminal revision?</h3>
<p>Quashing asks the High Court to end an FIR or case on grounds such as abuse of process. Criminal revision asks the High Court or Sessions Judge to examine the correctness, legality or propriety of a lower court's order. Revision does not lie against interlocutory orders.</p>

<h3>Does Section 528 BNSS apply to an FIR registered before 1 July 2024?</h3>
<p>The FIR is still investigated under the CrPC, and petitions about it are often described under Section 482 CrPC. The legal tests are the same either way. High Courts have taken different views on which number to use for later filings, so a petition is drafted with care on this point.</p>
